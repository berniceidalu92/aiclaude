# The Animal With Nine Brains That Dies for Its Babies

Her blood is blue. She has three hearts. Most of her nerve cells are not in her head, but spread through her arms, which can taste, touch and make decisions on their own.

She can change the colour and texture of her skin in less than a second. She can squeeze her whole body through a gap no wider than a coin.

And when she becomes a mother, she will stop eating, guard her eggs for months in the dark, and die the moment they hatch.

This is the giant Pacific octopus. And her life is one of the strangest, and most heartbreaking, stories in the ocean.

This is the cold, green water off the coast of British Columbia, in western Canada. Here, forests of giant kelp rise from the rocky seafloor toward the light, swaying in the current. Sea lions hunt between the stalks. Wolf eels hide in the cracks. And on the bottom, among the boulders, something is watching.

At first, it looks like part of the rock. A lumpy, reddish-brown shape, wedged into a crevice.

Then two eyes open. And the rock begins to move.

We will call her Ruby. She is a giant Pacific octopus, the largest octopus in the world. From the tip of one arm to the tip of another, she is wider than a car. And yet, she has no bones at all.

The only hard part of her body is her beak, a sharp, parrot-like bite hidden at the centre of her arms. Anything her beak can fit through, the rest of her body can follow. A crack in the rocks. The mouth of a bottle. A gap no wider than a coin.

Inside her soft body is a design unlike almost any other animal on Earth.

She has three hearts. Two of them pump blood through her gills. The third pumps it around the rest of her body. And that blood is blue. Instead of iron, like ours, it carries oxygen using copper, which works better in cold, low-oxygen water.

And then there is her nervous system.

An octopus has around five hundred million nerve cells, about as many as a dog. But only about a third of them are in her central brain. The rest are spread through her eight arms. Each arm has its own cluster of nerves, controlling it almost like a small brain of its own.

That is why some people say an octopus has nine brains. One in her head, and one in every arm.

An arm can reach into a crack, feel around, decide what it has found, and grab it, while the rest of the octopus is busy doing something else.

And her arms do not just touch. They taste. Each of her suckers is packed with sensors that can taste chemicals in the water. When Ruby slides an arm into a hole in the rocks, she is tasting it at the same time, searching for food she cannot even see.

As the light fades, Ruby leaves her den to hunt.

She flows across the seafloor, arms rippling, skin shifting from red to brown to mottled grey as she passes over sand, rock and weed. Every few seconds she pauses, and her arms spread out, slipping into every crack and crevice around her.

One arm finds something. A red rock crab, tucked under a ledge.

Ruby does not hesitate. The arm wraps around it. Then another. The crab snaps its claws, but there is nothing hard to grab onto. In seconds, it is pulled into the web of her arms, toward her beak.

She bites, and injects saliva that paralyses the crab and begins to soften its flesh. Then she carries it back to her den to eat. Later, she will push the empty shells out of the entrance, leaving a little pile of leftovers outside her front door.

But the hunter can quickly become the hunted.

A shadow slides across the kelp above her. A Steller sea lion, a huge, fast predator, and one of the few animals that hunts large octopuses.

Ruby sees it. And her skin changes in an instant.

Her reddish colour drains away. Bumps and spikes rise across her body, matching the rough texture of the rocks. In less than a second, she has turned into a mottled grey boulder, covered in weed-like frills.

Her skin is covered in thousands of tiny colour cells, each one a sac of pigment that she can open or close with muscles. Below them, other cells reflect light, making her shimmer, glow white or turn dark. And muscles in her skin can raise bumps and spikes, changing her texture as well as her colour.

The sea lion swims past. Then it turns, and comes back.

It has seen something. It dives straight at the rocks.

Ruby explodes off the seafloor. A cloud of black ink bursts out of her body, swirling into a dark, octopus-sized blob in the water. The sea lion snaps at the shape. It is only ink.

Ruby is already gone, shooting backwards through the water, arms trailing behind her like a comet, jet-propelled by water forced out of her body.

She squeezes into a narrow crack in the rocks, so small it seems impossible, and pulls her arms in after her.

The sea lion circles twice. Then gives up, and swims away into the kelp.

When Ruby finally creeps out, one of her arms is shorter than the others. In the struggle, she lost the tip.

But it will grow back. An octopus can regrow a lost arm, complete with suckers and nerves.

Here is the strangest part of her disguise. As far as scientists can tell, an octopus is colour-blind. Her eyes have only one type of light-sensing cell. And yet she can match the colours around her almost perfectly.

How she does it is still a mystery. One idea is that her skin itself may help, because scientists have found light-sensitive proteins in octopus skin, the same kind of proteins that eyes use to see.

Ruby may be, in a small way, seeing with her skin.

An octopus is also one of the most intelligent animals without a backbone. In aquariums, octopuses have learned to unscrew jars, solve puzzles, and escape from their tanks. Some seem to recognise individual people, and treat them differently.

Their relatives around the world have their own tricks. In Indonesia, the veined octopus collects empty coconut shells and carries them across the seafloor, then hides inside them, one of the few known examples of tool use in an invertebrate. And the mimic octopus can disguise itself as a lionfish, a sea snake, or a flat fish lying on the sand.

With a brain like that, you might expect an octopus to live a long time.

But it does not.

A giant Pacific octopus lives only about three to five years. And it breeds only once.

In the spring, a male finds Ruby's den.

He approaches carefully. Among octopuses, a meeting like this can be dangerous. A female sometimes attacks, and even eats, a male.

He reaches out one special arm, longer than the others, with a groove along its underside. And with it, he passes her a packet of sperm.

Then he leaves.

He will never see her again. Within a few months, he will start to fade. He will stop eating, lose weight, and wander out into the open water. And he will die.

For Ruby, the last chapter of her life is about to begin.

She finds a den deep in the rocks, a small cave with a rocky ceiling. And there, over several weeks, she lays her eggs.

Tens of thousands of them. Each one no bigger than a grain of rice. She weaves them together into long strings, and hangs them from the ceiling of the den, like bunches of tiny white grapes.

And then, she begins to guard them.

She will not leave this den again.

Day and night, for around six months, Ruby stays with her eggs. She runs her arms over them gently, cleaning off any dirt or algae. She blows a constant stream of fresh water across them, so the growing babies inside get enough oxygen.

She does not hunt. She does not eat. Not once.

When a hungry crab creeps into the den, she drives it out. When a sea star slides toward the entrance, she pushes it away. Every intruder is fought off, even as she grows weaker.

Week after week, her body begins to change. Her skin, once rich red, fades to a pale, patchy grey. Her eyes cloud over. She grows thin. Her arms move more slowly.

She is starving herself to death, to keep her babies alive.

In the deep sea, a different kind of octopus has been filmed guarding her eggs for four and a half years. The longest brooding period ever recorded for any animal on Earth. For all that time, scientists never saw her eat.

Why would a mother do this?

The answer lies in a small gland near the octopus's eyes. When an octopus breeds, this gland starts sending out chemical signals that seem to switch off her appetite and set her body on a path toward death.

Decades ago, scientists discovered that when this gland was removed from a brooding octopus, she stopped guarding her eggs, started eating again, and lived for months longer.

It seems the octopus is built with a kind of self-destruct switch. Once she becomes a mother, her body is programmed to give everything it has to one single generation, and nothing to herself.

After six months, the eggs begin to move.

Inside each one, a tiny octopus has formed. You can see its eyes. Its little arms. Its colour cells flickering on and off.

And one by one, they hatch.

Ruby, so weak now she can barely move, does one last thing. She blows water across the egg strings, gently, again and again, helping the babies break free and pushing them out of the den into the open sea.

Thousands of tiny octopuses, each smaller than a grain of rice, drift up toward the surface like a cloud of glitter.

Most of them will be eaten within days. Only a handful will survive to grow up. But one day, a few of them will return to these rocks, giant, clever and strange, just like their mother.

Ruby stays in the den.

She has given them everything she had. And soon after the last egg hatches, she dies.

It is one of the saddest stories in nature. But it is also one of the most remarkable. An animal with blue blood, three hearts and a brain in every arm, an animal clever enough to escape from almost anything, uses its very last breath to push its babies out into the world.

Today, giant Pacific octopuses are still found along the coasts of the North Pacific, from California to Japan. But warming seas, pollution and fishing are changing the cold waters they depend on.

Perhaps the creator made the octopus to remind us that intelligence is not only about living long. Sometimes, the cleverest thing an animal can do is give its whole life for the next one.

Tonight, in the cold dark water off British Columbia, a tiny octopus no bigger than a grain of rice drifts among the plankton, its colour cells flickering in the moonlight.

Its mother is gone.

But somewhere inside it, already, are three hearts, blue blood, and nine brains.
