The end of autumn, in the Spiti Valley. A herd of ibex is crossing a cliff face, single file, on a ledge as wide as a hand.

A golden eagle is circling high above them. It does not pick the biggest. It picks the smallest, the young kid at the very back of the line, on the narrowest part of the ledge.

It climbs higher, until the sun is directly behind it. Then it folds its wings and drops.

Three hundred meters in a few seconds. The kid never sees it coming.

The talons hit its back. The kid staggers toward the edge, hooves scraping, and somehow stays on the rock.

The eagle swings out over the valley, turns, and comes again. And again. Each time, the kid is pushed a little closer to the drop.

On the third dive, the mother rears up on her hind legs between them, horns swinging, and the eagle has to pull away.

It circles twice more. Then it gives up, and rides the wind back down the valley.

Why does an eagle hunt like this? Because a young ibex is far too heavy to carry. So it does not try. It tries to knock the kid off the cliff, and then eats at the bottom. Here, the mountain does the killing.

And why didn't the kid fall? Look at its hooves. Hard edges for gripping, and a soft rubbery pad in the middle that clings to rock like a climbing shoe. An ibex kid can climb cliffs within days of being born.

The kid presses itself against its mother's legs. She is the one who chose this ledge. We will call her Nima. And her kid, Tashi.

Above them, on a pinnacle of rock, a huge male stands watching. Horns over a meter long, ridged like the blade of a saw, swept back over his shoulders. He is old, and scarred, and he has ruled this cliff for years. We will call him Kala.

How do you survive a winter where everything hunts you, and the only safe place is a cliff that can kill you?

Wild Himalaya. The goats that fight snow leopards.

The eagle has taught Nima one thing. The high ledges are not safe for Tashi. So she leads him down, onto the lower slopes.

And straight into the rut.

It is the start of winter, the season when the males fight for the right to breed. And a young male has come down off the mountain to take Kala's herd.

He does not pick a weak male. He picks the one with the biggest horns. Because to win the herd, he has to beat the king.

He walks along the cliff edge toward Kala, side on, chin high, showing off the length of his horns. Kala turns side on too.

Then the young male charges. Kala meets him at a run, across the scree, straight toward the edge.

They rear up on their hind legs. Hang there for a heartbeat. And crash down, horns first, with all their weight behind them.

The crack echoes across the valley. Again. And again.

The young male is bigger and stronger. He drives Kala back, one step, two steps, until Kala's back hooves are on the very edge of the cliff.

Then Kala twists his head, hooks his horns under the young male's, and throws him sideways.

The young male's hooves slip on the loose scree. He slides. Tumbles. Twenty meters down the slope in a shower of rocks.

He gets up. Bleeding from a gash on his leg. And limps away down the mountain, alone.

Why fight on a cliff edge? Because the edge is part of the fight. Every ibex is trying to push the other off balance, and on a cliff, one slip is enough. And why don't they crack their skulls? An ibex has a double layer of bone over its brain, like a crash helmet. The ridges on the horns lock together, so the blows go into the neck and shoulders, not the head.

But the fight has cost both of them. During the rut, the males barely eat. By the end of it, Kala will have lost almost a fifth of his body weight, just as winter begins.

The beaten young male limps down into a gully, alone, below a wall of rock.

And someone has been watching the fight from the ridge above.

A snow leopard. A grey ghost with black rosettes and a tail almost as long as her body. We will call her Dolma.

She does not pick the herd. She picks the young male. He is limping. He is alone. And he is standing below her, at the bottom of a cliff, exactly where a snow leopard wants its prey.

She creeps along the ridge, belly to the rock, her smoky coat disappearing against the stone. Thirty meters above him. Twenty.

Then she launches.

The young male sees her in the air and runs, the only way open. Straight down the mountain.

She lands behind him, and the chase goes straight down a slope so steep, a human could not walk on it.

Leaping from rock to rock, ten meters at a time. Her tail swinging like a rudder. Closing.

She catches him at full speed. Claws in his shoulders. Teeth at his neck.

And they both go over the edge.

Two animals, tumbling down two hundred meters of cliff, rolling over and over in an avalanche of rocks and snow. Neither can let go. Neither can stop.

They hit the bottom, and roll apart.

For a second, neither moves.

Then the young male staggers up, shakes the snow off, and runs. Up a wall of rock where nothing can follow him.

Dolma limps away into the boulders. Hungry.

Why chase him down the cliff, when the fall could kill her too? Because that is how snow leopards hunt. They attack from above, so the prey has to run downhill, faster than it can control. Most of the time, the prey falls first. And her tail is the only thing that lets her turn at that speed.

But a snow leopard only makes a large kill about once every week or two. And Dolma has not killed in nine days. Because she is not hunting for one.

In a cave below the cliffs, two grown cubs are waiting. Almost as big as she is. And just as hungry.

The next morning, three snow leopards come back for the herd.

This time, they want the easiest target on the mountain. Tashi. The smallest. The lightest. The one in his first winter.

Dolma spreads them out. One cub below the herd. One cub on the far side. Dolma above, on the ridge, cutting off the high cliffs.

The cubs crawl in through the boulders. Fifty meters. Thirty. One of them moves too soon.

The herd explodes up the cliff. Tashi runs, but he is too slow, and the first cub is right behind him.

Nima turns back.

She leaps down to a narrow ledge, puts Tashi behind her against the rock, and lowers her horns.

The first cub lunges. She rams it square in the chest, and it slides backwards off the ledge onto the scree below.

The second cub comes from the side. She spins, hooves skidding, and it backs off snarling.

Then Dolma drops onto the ledge above them.

Three snow leopards. One mother ibex. And one kid.

Dolma reaches down with one huge paw, and her claws scrape the rock a hand's width from Tashi's back.

Nima rears up into her face. And in that second, Tashi does the only thing he can. He climbs.

Straight up a wall of bare rock. Not a ledge. Not a crack. Just rock. Hooves finding holds nobody else could see.

Five meters up. Ten. Out of reach.

Nima follows him. And three snow leopards are left on the ledge below, staring up at two ibex standing on a vertical wall.

Why did the leopards fail? Because the ledge was narrow. Only one of them could reach Nima at a time, and one at a time, she could fight them. And the cubs were too young. A grown snow leopard waits until the last moment. The cub that moved too soon gave the whole herd a warning.

But the leopards were not the biggest danger on the mountain. That night, the sky turns black. And the snow comes.

It falls for three days. When it stops, the high slopes are buried under two meters of snow. There is nothing left to eat up here.

So the ibex do what they do every winter. They go down. Down toward the valley, to the slopes where the sun melts the snow.

And to get there, they have to cross the ice.

A steep slope of frozen snow, shining like glass, with a long drop to the valley floor below. The herd crosses it in single file, hooves cutting into the crust.

And this time, there are two eagles.

The first eagle that hunted Tashi has found a mate. And they have learned what works on this mountain. They both pick Tashi. The smallest. The last in the line. Crossing the steepest part of the ice.

They split up. One circles high, in the sun. The other drops low, and comes in fast along the slope, right at Tashi's head.

Tashi flinches away from it. And as he does, the second eagle hits him from above.

Talons in his back. Wings beating around his face. His hooves skid on the ice.

And he slides.

Down the frozen slope on his side, faster and faster, the two eagles diving at him as he goes.

Nima does not think. She throws herself after him, sliding down the ice on her hooves, sending up a spray of snow.

Tashi slams into a snowdrift at the bottom and stops. The eagles swing in for another strike, and Nima reaches him first. She rears up over him, horns slashing at the air, until both eagles pull away.

One eagle could not knock Tashi off a ledge. Two eagles together nearly did. Why does it work better with two? Because one eagle distracts, and the other strikes. A kid can watch one attacker. It cannot watch two. Hunting in pairs, golden eagles have knocked even adult goats off a mountain.

Tashi is alive. But he and Nima are now alone, at the bottom of the slope. In deep snow. Far from the cliffs, and far from the herd.

Exactly where the wolves are waiting.

Four wolves. Lean, grey, built for the cold. They have been following the herd for days, waiting for one of them to come down.

They pick Nima. She is struggling across an open slope of fresh snow with Tashi behind her, alone, far from the herd, and she is sinking to her chest with every step.

The wolves do not run at first. They walk. In a line, one behind the other, along the top of the frozen snow. Closing the gap. One hundred meters. Fifty.

Nima sees them. She plunges toward the rocks at the bottom of the slope. But every leap sinks her deeper.

The wolves start to run. And they do not sink.

The lead wolf reaches her first and grabs her back leg. She kicks, spins in the snow, and hooks it with her horns. It rolls away.

A second wolf goes for Tashi. Nima throws herself between them, and the wolf's teeth close on her shoulder instead.

The other two close in from the sides.

And then Kala, who has followed them down from the herd, comes down the slope like an avalanche.

He hits the first wolf at full speed, horns first, and sends it tumbling into a snowdrift. The others scatter. And for a few seconds, the way to the rocks is open.

Nima drags herself out of the snow and runs. Tashi on her heels. Kala last, horns lowered, backing up the rocks.

Four wolves. One mother ibex. And still, the ibex win.

Why could the wolves run, when Nima could not? Look at their feet. A wolf's paws are wide, and spread its weight across the snow like snowshoes. An ibex has small, sharp hooves that punch straight through. On rock, an ibex is the master. In deep snow, it is helpless.

And why run for the rocks? Because wolves cannot climb. On a cliff, a wolf is just an animal standing at the bottom, looking up.

The four wolves stand at the foot of the rocks and lift their heads. And they howl.

From across the valley, more wolves answer.

The pack comes together that night. Eight wolves, and every one of them starving. The deep snow has buried the valley, and there is nothing left to hunt.

Except a kill that is not theirs.

Down on the frozen river, Dolma has finally caught a blue sheep. She and her cubs are feeding on it in the moonlight.

The wolves pick the cubs. Not Dolma. The two young leopards feeding on the edge of the kill, away from their mother.

They spread out across the frozen river and creep in from the dark. A snow leopard cannot roar. It does not even hear them coming.

Then the pack charges.

Eight wolves pour across the ice. The cubs scatter. One runs straight into two wolves and is knocked flat on the ice.

Dolma leaps onto the first wolf. Rolls it over. Slashes another across the face.

But there are eight of them, and they keep coming. Two at her back. Two at her sides. Teeth at her tail.

She backs away. The cub on the ice scrambles up and runs to her. Together, the three leopards retreat up into the rocks.

And the wolves take the kill.

Why give it up? Because a snow leopard cannot afford a single injury. A torn leg means she cannot climb. And a snow leopard who cannot climb, cannot hunt. One leopard against eight wolves is not a fight she can win, so she does not try.

But one blue sheep cannot feed eight wolves. By dawn there is nothing left but bones. And they are hungrier than ever.

They need something big. And there is only one animal on the mountain big enough to feed them all.

Kala.

The grey female has chosen her target: Kala, and only Kala. She has chosen her ground: the open slope below the cliffs. And she has chosen her moment: dusk, when he comes down to feed.

Kala is old. The rut has burned away a fifth of his body. And every evening, he walks a little farther from the cliffs to find grass under the snow.

The next evening, he walks farther than ever.

The wolves have been watching him for two days. They have learned his path. Down the gully at sunset. Out onto the open slope. Back to the cliffs before dark.

That afternoon, they move into place. Four wolves climb high, and slip one by one into the snow above the slope, between Kala and the high cliffs. Four more circle low, downwind, and lie flat in the drifts below.

Then they wait. An hour. Two. The sun drops behind the peaks.

He does not see them. Eight grey shapes, buried in the snow, all around the slope where he feeds.

He lowers his head to feed. And the grey female stands up.

The pack comes from above and below at once.

Kala runs for the high cliffs. But four wolves are already there, cutting him off.

He swings away across the slope, the snow dragging at his legs. Eight wolves running across the top of it.

He reaches the ridge. Runs along it. And there, at the end, is a ledge no wider than a doorway, hanging over a two-hundred-meter drop.

He backs onto it.

Four wolves on his left. Four on his right.

The first wolf lunges. He drops his head, and a meter of curved horn sweeps it off its feet.

A second comes from the other side. He spins on the narrow rock, and the wolf takes his horns in the ribs.

Then the grey female comes. And she does not miss. Her teeth close on his back leg.

He staggers. His hooves slip toward the edge. Two more wolves rush in behind her.

Kala does not try to fight them all.

He turns toward the drop, and jumps.

Five meters down, onto a crack in the cliff face no wider than his hooves. He lands on it. Sways. And holds.

The grey female lunges after him, and stops at the edge. A young wolf behind her does not stop. It goes over, and lands in the snow far below.

Seven wolves stand on the ledge, staring down. And Kala stands on the cliff beneath them, where no wolf on Earth can follow.

So how did one old ibex beat eight wolves?

Look at the final hunt again.

The wolves did everything right. They waited until the snow drove him down. They caught him in the open. They cut him off from the high cliffs.

But they could not stop him reaching the ledge. And on a ledge as wide as a doorway, eight wolves are not a pack. They are one wolf at a time.

And then, the cliff. A wolf's greatest weapon is its numbers. An ibex's greatest weapon is the mountain itself. When the pack finally reached him, he simply stepped off it.

One eagle could not take Tashi. Two eagles could not. One snow leopard could not take the young male. Three could not take Nima. Four wolves could not. Eight wolves could not.

The mountain that can kill them is the only thing that keeps them alive.

Kala stays on the cliff all night. By morning, the wolves are gone, and he climbs slowly back up to the herd.

Nima is there. And Tashi, the kid who started this story clinging to a ledge as wide as a hand, has survived his first winter on the mountain.

In spring, the snow melts off the high slopes, and the herd climbs back up to the cliffs. And somewhere above them, Dolma and her cubs are watching.

The Himalaya is one of the last strongholds of the snow leopard. There may be as few as four thousand left in the wild.

But the mountains are changing. The winters are getting warmer, the snow line is climbing, and the glaciers are shrinking. The ibex, the leopards and the wolves are all being squeezed into less and less of the mountain.

Perhaps the creator gave the ibex the cliffs and the wolves the snow, so that neither would ever have it all, to show us that even the strongest pack can be beaten by the right ground.

Somewhere in the Spiti Valley tonight, an old ibex stands on a ledge above the world, the scars of eight wolves on his legs.

The mountain is still waiting for spring.

And so is he.
