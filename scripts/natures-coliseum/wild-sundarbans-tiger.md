# WILD SUNDARBANS | The Tigers That Swim With Sharks

Jaws slam shut on her shoulder. The crocodile rolls, and a tigress vanishes under the brown water while her cubs scream.

A king cobra rears up, hood spread, eye to eye with a cub. One bite could kill an elephant. He cannot move.

Two tigresses rear up, claws raking faces, jaws locked at each other's throats. Then one forces the other under the water.

A wall of water smashes through the forest in the dark. Three tigers swimming for their lives. When it passes, only two.

A cub alone on a raft. A fin slicing closer. The raft tips, his legs drop in, and the shark glides beneath him.

A wounded mother sinking. Her son pushing her to shore. And behind them, the crocodile is back. This is how it began.

Twice a day, this forest disappears.

The sea pushes in. The rivers run backwards. Mud becomes channel, and channel becomes sea.

Anything that cannot swim here does not live here for long.

Tigers are supposed to avoid water. These ones were born in it.

At the edge of a channel, a tigress stands with the tide rising around her paws. Across the water, a hundred meters away, is the island where she needs to be.

Below the surface, something is waiting. Something that has been waiting for tides like this for sixty years.

She steps in anyway.

Most predators choose where they fight. Here, the tide chooses for you.

On the island she is leaving, fresh claw marks have appeared on the trees. Higher than hers. Newer than hers.

Someone else wants this forest.

She swims with her head low, eyes fixed on the far bank. Behind her, two small heads bob in the brown water, paddling hard to keep up.

Her cubs.

The crocodile is below them. The stranger is behind them. And the tide is still rising.

So how do you raise a family in a forest that drowns twice a day?

This is the story of a mother and the water that tests her. It is as though the creator took the most powerful cat on Earth and placed it in the one forest where strength alone is never enough.

Wild Sundarbans. The tigers that swim with sharks.

The Sundarbans. The largest mangrove forest on Earth.

Ten thousand square kilometres of islands, mud and roots, where the Ganges and the Brahmaputra meet the Bay of Bengal, shared between India and Bangladesh.

Here, the tide can rise and fall by several meters in a single day.

Saltwater crocodiles patrol the channels. Bull sharks follow the tide upriver. King cobras hunt among the roots.

And living among all of it are some of the only tigers on Earth that hunt between the tides.

This is Rani.

A Bengal tigress. Lean, dark-striped, her legs stained grey with mud.

Tigers in the Sundarbans are smaller than tigers in the dry forests. There is no room here for extra weight. Every kilo has to swim.

She can cross channels several kilometres wide. She hunts deer, wild boar, monkeys, fish, even crabs. Whatever the tide leaves behind.

Beside her, two cubs. Ten months old.

The bigger one is her son. We will call him Ravi. First into the water. First into trouble.

The smaller one is her daughter, Nila. She watches everything before she does anything.

Tiger cubs stay with their mothers for up to two years. In this forest, every one of those days begins with the same question.

Where is the tide?

Low tide. The forest floor rises out of the water like a secret.

Grey mud, shining in the sun. Thousands of fiddler crabs, each waving one giant claw. Mudskippers, fish that walk, flipping across the surface.

Rani walks out onto the mud. The cubs follow, sinking to their elbows.

This is the first lesson. At low tide, the forest feeds you.

Ravi pounces on a crab. The crab grabs his nose. He leaps backwards into the mud and comes up grey from ear to ear.

Nila watches. Then she pins a crab with one paw and eats it.

But the mud has a timer.

Already the water is creeping back into the channels. Quietly. A few centimetres a minute.

Ravi has wandered far out, following the mudskippers into the roots.

The roots here grow upwards, out of the mud. Thousands of them, sharp as stakes. The mangroves breathe through them.

And something else lives between them.

A sound. A low hiss, like air escaping a tyre.

Out of the roots rises a king cobra.

The longest venomous snake on Earth. It lifts a third of its body off the ground. Hood spread. Eyes level with Ravi's.

Ravi freezes. For once in his life, he does not move first.

A single bite carries enough venom to kill an elephant.

The cobra sways. Ravi's ears go flat.

Then a shadow falls across the mud. Rani.

She does not attack. She roars.

The cobra holds its ground for one long second. Then it folds back into the roots and is gone.

Rani turns and walks away. Ravi follows, very close this time.

In this forest, curiosity is a luxury. Only the careful get to grow up.

The tide comes in. The forest floor vanishes. And the hunting begins.

Above the water, rhesus monkeys feed in the canopy, dropping leaves and fruit as they go.

Below them, a herd of spotted deer follows, eating what falls. The monkeys watch from above. The deer listen from below.

Together, they make an alarm system that is almost impossible to beat.

Almost.

Rani moves through water up to her shoulders. Only her head shows above the surface, flat and striped like driftwood.

The deer do not look at the water. They are watching the forest.

She closes in. Thirty meters. Twenty. A monkey shrieks, but not at her.

She rises out of the tide in one surge. The deer scatter. One stag is a step too slow.

She brings it down in the shallows.

She drags it up onto a mud bank. For the first time in days, the cubs eat until their bellies are tight.

But blood in the water travels with the tide.

That night, a roar rolls across the island. Deep. And very close.

The claw marks were not a warning. They were an introduction.

Out of the dark walks a tigress. Bigger than Rani. Older. One ear torn half away in some fight long ago.

We will call her Torn Ear.

She does not want the kill. She wants the island.

Rani stands over her cubs and snarls. Torn Ear circles, slow, testing.

A mother with cubs cannot afford a fight to the end. Win or lose, an injured mother means starving cubs.

So Rani makes the hardest decision a predator can make.

She leaves.

She leaves the deer. She leaves the island. And she leads her cubs to the water's edge.

The only way out is across.

The channel is four hundred meters wide. The tide is running. And it is getting dark.

Rani steps in first. Ravi follows without waiting. Nila hesitates at the edge, then plunges after them.

Three heads in the water, moving slowly against the current.

On the far bank, a long grey shape slides off the mud. Without a sound.

A saltwater crocodile. The largest reptile on Earth, with the strongest bite ever measured in a living animal.

It does not swim toward them. It sinks.

That is the most frightening thing a crocodile can do.

Halfway across, Nila is falling behind. Her strokes are getting shorter.

The water in front of her bulges.

The crocodile comes up beneath her, jaws open.

Rani turns in the water and hurls herself between them.

The jaws close on her shoulder.

The crocodile rolls. Tiger and crocodile vanish under the brown water.

The cubs paddle in circles, crying.

One second. Three. Five.

Rani bursts up, raking her claws across the crocodile's eyes. It lets go.

The eyes are the one place a crocodile has no armour.

She drives the cubs toward the far bank, pushing Nila with her head, bleeding into the water.

They crawl out onto the mud as the last light fades.

They made it. But a wounded tiger in the Sundarbans leaves a trail anyone can follow.

And far out over the Bay of Bengal, the sky is turning a colour none of them has ever seen.

The wind arrives first.

It comes off the sea in the middle of the night, bending the mangroves until their tops touch the water.

Then the rain. Not falling. Flying sideways.

Cyclones form over the Bay of Bengal almost every year. Most miss the forest. This one does not.

Rani has found high ground. A raised bank beneath a tangle of roots. The cubs press against her belly.

And then the sea comes in.

A storm surge. A wall of water pushed ahead of the cyclone, meters higher than any tide.

The whole island goes under.

The family is swimming in the dark. No banks. No shore. Only black water and the scream of the wind.

Rani keeps her body upstream of the cubs, shielding them from the current.

Nila hooks her claws into a floating log.

Ravi does not.

A wave breaks over all three of them. When Rani surfaces, there is one head beside her.

Only one.

She calls. A short, urgent sound. Again. And again.

The wind takes every call away.

Somewhere out in the flooded forest, a ten-month-old cub is alone in the water.

And the sea is still rising.

Morning. The storm has passed.

The forest looks like a different world. Trees snapped in half. Islands reshaped. Channels where there was land yesterday.

Ravi is clinging to a raft of broken branches, drifting down a wide channel far from where he started.

He is soaked. Shivering. Exhausted.

The storm pushed the sea far up the rivers. And it brought visitors with it.

A fin breaks the surface. Then disappears.

A bull shark. One of the few sharks on Earth that can live in fresh water. They follow the salt up these rivers, hunting anything that falls in.

The fin appears again. Closer.

Ravi's raft tips. He slips. His back legs drop into the water.

He scrambles back up, claws digging into the bark.

The shark passes beneath him. Close enough that the water swirls around his paws.

A cub on a raft has nowhere to run. And nothing to fight with but noise.

So he does the one thing his mother would do. He roars.

A tiny, cracked roar. Barely a roar at all.

The shark circles once more. And then it turns away, back toward deeper water.

Perhaps it was the noise. Perhaps it was luck.

The raft drifts into the roots of a half-drowned island, and Ravi drags himself onto the mud.

He is alive. But he is alone. And he is standing in a territory that is not his mother's.

By afternoon, hunger arrives. It always does.

There is no mother here to bring down a deer. No kill to share. Only mud, roots, and a tide that is starting to fall.

And as the water drains away, the forest floor appears again. Grey mud, shining. Thousands of fiddler crabs, waving their claws.

Ravi has seen this before.

He crouches. He waits. He does not pounce at the first crab that moves, the way he did a few weeks ago.

He watches. The way his sister watched.

Then one paw comes down. Fast. Precise.

He eats his first meal alone.

It is only a crab. But it is the first lesson, learned.

Out in the channel, a water monitor swims past, two meters long, tasting the air with its tongue. It turns toward the cub on the mud.

Ravi stands up and spreads his paws wide. Ears back. Teeth showing.

The lizard thinks about it. Then it keeps swimming.

A few weeks ago, he would have run.

Night falls. Ravi curls up in the roots, alone for the first time in his life, listening to the tide come back in around him.

Every sound in the dark could be a crocodile. Or a shark. Or something worse.

He does not sleep.

Miles away, Rani is searching.

She swims channel after channel, Nila on her back when the water gets too wide.

She stops on every bank. Sniffs every root. Calls into every silence.

A cub knows his mother's voice. But the storm has scattered every scent in the forest.

On the second day, she finds something. A tangle of broken branches, caught against a bank. Tiger hair in the bark. Claw marks, small ones.

His raft.

She follows the channel downstream, swimming harder now. Nila clings to her back, soaked and silent.

And she is not the only one on the move.

The cyclone has destroyed territories all across the delta. Every tiger in the forest is searching for new ground.

Including Torn Ear.

And the island where Ravi has washed up is exactly the kind of high ground she is looking for.

Ravi smells her before he sees her.

Not his mother. Bigger. Older. Wrong.

Torn Ear steps out of the roots.

She looks at the cub on the mud. Her ears go back.

To a tigress claiming new ground, a stranger's cub is not a child. It is future competition.

Ravi backs away until his tail touches the water. Behind him, the channel. In front of him, a tiger three times his size.

Torn Ear lowers her head and stalks forward.

And then, from the water, a call.

Short. Urgent. The sound he has heard every night of his life.

Rani comes out of the channel like the tide itself.

She does not stop to snarl. She does not circle. She hits Torn Ear at full speed, shoulder to shoulder.

The two tigresses rise onto their hind legs, locked together, roaring.

Claws rake across faces. Jaws snap at throats. They crash into the mud and roll into the shallows.

Torn Ear is bigger. She is heavier. But she is fighting for an island.

Rani is fighting for her son.

The fight spills into the water. Spray. Mud. Blood. The wound on Rani's shoulder tears open again.

Torn Ear gets a grip on Rani's neck and forces her under.

Ravi charges. A ten-month-old cub, throwing himself at a grown tiger's back legs.

It is not enough to hurt her. But it is enough to distract her.

Torn Ear's grip slips. Rani twists free and clamps down on her rival's torn ear.

Torn Ear screams and breaks away.

She backs off. Stops. Looks at the mother and the cub standing side by side in the mud.

Then she turns and walks into the forest.

Some fights are won by the strongest. This one was won by the one with the most to lose.

Rani lies down in the mud. Breathing hard. Bleeding from the shoulder and the neck.

Ravi presses his face against hers.

But the forest is not finished with them.

Nila is still on the far side of the channel, hidden on high ground where Rani left her. And the tide is turning.

To reach her, they have to cross back. Through the same water, at the same hour, where the crocodile hunts.

Rani steps in. Slowly. Her strokes are weak. Her shoulder will not work properly.

Halfway across, she begins to sink lower in the water.

Ravi is beside her. And he does what she has done for him all his life.

He swims on her downstream side. He pushes against her with his shoulder. He keeps her pointed at the bank.

Behind them, a long shape slides off the mud and into the river.

The crocodile.

Ravi sees it. He does not panic. He keeps swimming. Keeps pushing.

Six meters from shore. Three.

Rani's paws touch mud. Ravi scrambles out behind her. The crocodile surfaces where they were, a moment too late.

On the bank, Nila comes running out of the roots.

The family is together again.

A cub who could barely swim a channel a few weeks ago has just carried his mother across one.

So how do you raise a family in a forest that drowns twice a day?

Not by being the strongest. Out here, even the strongest have to swim.

Not by avoiding the water. Nothing here can.

You teach them to read the tide before you teach them to hunt. And you teach them that in the water, no one makes it across alone.

But the Sundarbans is changing.

The sea here is rising faster than the global average. Whole islands have already vanished beneath the waves. Cyclones are growing stronger. Fresh water is turning to salt.

The mangroves are a wall between the sea and millions of people living behind them. They break the storms. They hold the land together.

And the tigers are the forest's guardians. Where the tigers are protected, the forest is protected too.

There is hope. Tiger numbers in the Sundarbans have held steady, and in recent counts, they have even risen.

Perhaps the creator placed the tiger at the edge of the sea to show us something. That even the most powerful life on Earth depends on the forest holding its ground.

Somewhere in the Sundarbans tonight, a tigress lies on a mud bank, her two cubs pressed against her as the tide comes in.

She is wounded. She is tired. She is still here.

All they ask is a forest that stays above the water.
