# WILD ALASKA | The Bears That Fight for the Waterfall

A male rears up against a spruce trunk, claws ripping the bark, and a tiny cub clings just above his open jaws.

A cub swept off her feet by the current, tumbling toward the rapids, as a giant male wades in after her.

A wolf and a grizzly mother, face to face in the shallows, teeth bared over the same salmon. Neither one backs down.

Knocked down once. Knocked down twice. A mother bear rises out of the river again, roaring into the face of a giant.

Two giants rise three meters tall in the white water and crash together, jaws open. Every bear on the river stops fishing.

Jaws locked at the top of the falls. Claws raking. Then the king of the waterfall goes over the edge. This is how it began.

Below the falls, two male grizzlies want the same rock. Neither one lowers his head.

They rise onto their hind legs, three meters tall, and slam together. Jaws open. Water exploding around them.

Bite. Swipe. Bite. A paw the size of a dinner plate crashes into a skull.

Then one slips on the wet stone, and it is over. He limps away bleeding. The winner climbs onto the rock.

All around them stand fifty more grizzly bears. The biggest predators on land, almost shoulder to shoulder. Any one of them could kill any other.

So why are only these two fighting?

Because every bear on this river knows its rank.

The biggest males take the best places. The smaller bears give way. Most arguments here are settled with a stare, a lowered head, a single step backwards.

A young bear wades toward a big male's fishing rock. Too close. The male swings one paw, and the young bear tumbles into the water, scrambles up, and backs away.

One blow. No second one needed.

On this river, the fight is usually over before it starts.

So why do some bears still fight until one of them bleeds?

Because rank only works when the difference is obvious. When two bears are nearly the same size, nobody knows who should step back.

And the only way to find out is what you just saw.

This summer, two bears that size are on the same river. And they both want the same spot.

A single ledge of rock at the top of a waterfall. Barely wide enough for one bear to stand on.

Why would the most powerful animals in North America risk their lives for one ledge of rock?

This is the story of a mother, a river, and the spot every bear would kill for. It is as though the creator put a feast in front of the hungriest animals on Earth, and then made it just small enough that not everyone could reach it.

Wild Alaska. The bears that fight for the waterfall.

Katmai, on the Alaska Peninsula. A land of volcanoes, lakes and rivers, where one of the largest salmon runs on Earth comes home every summer.

And the bears come with it.

Down on the riverbank, a mother steps out of the forest. Thin. Ribs showing under her coat.

Behind her, two cubs. Five months old. Born in a den in the middle of winter. This is the first summer they have ever seen.

The bigger one is a male. We will call him Bo. The smaller one, a female, Fern.

And their mother, Tula.

They have barely reached the water when a male lifts his head from the river. He has seen the cubs. He wades straight toward them.

Tula does not wait. She charges, ears flat, huffing, and stops one stride from his face, jaws snapping.

The male stops. Weighs it. And turns away. For now.

Why would a mother bring tiny cubs to a river full of the bears most likely to kill them?

Because she has no choice.

For six months, Tula lay in a den on the mountain. No food. No water. Nursing two cubs on nothing but her own body.

She has lost almost a third of her weight.

The salmon are here for a few weeks. If she cannot put that weight back on before winter, none of them will wake up in spring.

But the river is full of males. And to a male bear, cubs are not just a nuisance.

Why would a male bear kill a cub that is not even a threat?

Because a mother with cubs will not mate. Take away her cubs, and she will.

It is the cruellest rule on this river. And Tula has seen it happen.

So where does a mother fish when every good place belongs to a male?

At the bottom. Downstream. In the shallow riffles where nobody else wants to be.

Tula wades in. The cubs wait on the bank.

A salmon flashes past her legs. She lunges. Misses. Another one. Misses again.

Chasing fish in shallow water is like chasing shadows. Every lunge burns energy she does not have.

Then, finally, she pins one. Her first salmon of the summer. And a young bear is already splashing toward her to take it.

Tula drops low over the fish and swings. Her paw catches the thief across the face, and it spins away into the current.

On this river, catching a fish is only half the job. Keeping it is the other half.

So how does any bear here get fat?

Look upstream.

Below the falls, in a churning pool of white water, a huge male sits up to his chest. Eyes half closed. Not moving.

Is he resting? While the river is full of fish?

No. He is fishing.

Salmon swim into the pool to gather strength before they jump. They bump against his legs. Scraps from the bears above wash down to him.

He does not chase. He waits. And the river feeds him.

A young bear thinks the pool looks easy. It wades in beside him.

The male explodes out of the water. One lunge, jaws wide, and the young bear is gone, scrambling for the bank.

Sitting still is only possible when everyone is afraid of you.

But even that is not the best place on the river. So where is?

At the top.

The falls are not tall. Barely two meters. But to a salmon swimming upstream, they are a wall.

Thousands of fish gather below, and then they leap. Again and again, until they make it over.

And at the very top, standing on the lip of the falls, is the biggest bear on the river.

A male. Over four hundred and fifty kilos. A scar across his muzzle from some fight long ago.

We will call him Scar.

He does not lunge. He does not chase. He stands with his mouth open.

And a salmon jumps straight into it.

Why do the fish jump right at him? Why don't they just go around?

Because they can't.

The water only runs deep enough to jump from in a few places. And the best of them is right below the lip. The salmon have no choice but to leap where Scar is waiting.

A smaller male climbs the rocks beside him, edging onto the lip.

Scar does not even look at him. He swings. The smaller bear is knocked clean off the ledge and into the white water below.

The lip holds one bear. Scar decides which one.

So that is the spot. The ledge every bear on this river wants.

But why is it worth dying for?

Hold on to that question. Because on the far bank, someone else is asking it.

A young male walks out of the trees. Broad. Heavy. Nearly as big as Scar.

We will call him Ridge.

Last summer, Ridge would have stepped aside. Now he walks straight to the edge of the falls.

Will Scar give up his spot?

Scar turns his head. Lowers it. Stares.

Ridge stares back.

Neither bear moves.

On this river, a stare is supposed to settle it. This one doesn't.

Ridge takes one step forward. And Scar comes off the lip like an avalanche.

The two bears rise onto their hind legs, three meters tall, jaws open, crashing together in the white water.

They bite at necks and faces. They swing paws that can break a moose's back.

They slam into the rocks. They roll into the river.

Every bear on the falls stops fishing.

Then Ridge breaks away. Bleeding from the neck. He retreats downstream.

Scar climbs back onto the lip. Blood running from his own shoulder. He opens his mouth. Another salmon jumps in.

The king has kept his spot. But for how long?

And where does a beaten, hungry male go now?

Downstream. Toward the riffles. Toward the easiest food on the river.

Toward Tula's cubs.

Tula smells him before she sees him. She swings her head toward the bank where the cubs are waiting.

Ridge is already between her and them.

Can she reach them in time?

She charges out of the water, roaring. Ridge turns toward her. And the cubs scatter.

Bo bolts for the forest. Fern runs the wrong way. Into the river.

The current takes her off her feet.

She tumbles through the riffles, tiny head going under, coming up, going under again.

And Ridge wades in after her.

Who reaches her first?

Tula hits the water at full speed. She does not go for Fern. She goes for Ridge.

She slams into his side, a bear half his weight, and sinks her teeth into his neck.

Ridge swings at her. She takes the blow on her shoulder and keeps coming. Roaring. Biting. Never backing down.

Why would a smaller bear attack a male that could kill her?

Because a mother bear does not have to win. She only has to make the fight cost more than it is worth.

Ridge has already lost one fight today. He is bleeding. He is tired.

He backs away. Then he turns and splashes off downstream.

Tula wades into the current and pulls Fern out by the scruff of her neck. Soaked. Shaking. Alive.

But where is Bo?

Tula climbs the bank, calling. A low, deep huff.

From high above, a small voice answers.

Bo is high up a spruce tree, clinging to the trunk.

How did he get up there?

Brown bear cubs can climb like squirrels. Their claws are short and curved, made for gripping bark. By the time they are grown, they are too heavy to follow.

The safest place for a cub is the one place a big male cannot reach.

And a big male has followed them here. He stands up against the trunk, sniffing, claws scraping the bark below Bo.

Bo climbs higher. Tula charges up the bank and slams into the male from the side. He turns, snarls, and leaves.

The family is together. But Tula has lost a whole day of fishing. And the clock is running.

Every day the salmon run gets a little thinner. Every day the bears get a little fatter. Except her.

So how does a mother with two cubs ever get a turn at the good spots?

That evening, she tries something different.

She leads the cubs upstream, past the riffles, to the edge of the white-water pool below the falls.

The big male who sat there all day has gone to sleep in the forest. The pool is empty.

Tula wades in. And she does not chase.

She sits. Up to her chest. Just like he did.

A salmon bumps her leg. She grabs it.

Another one. She grabs it.

For the first time this summer, Tula eats until she cannot eat any more.

Why didn't she do this before?

Because the pool only empties when the big males leave. And they only leave when they are full. A mother has to wait for the edges of the day, the hours nobody else wants.

But the edges of the day belong to the young males too. One wades into the pool, straight at her.

Tula rises, water pouring off her shoulders, and meets him chest to chest. They swat. They bite. She drives him back to the bank.

A mother cannot win the river. But she can win a pool, for an evening.

Night falls. And the next morning, there is a new problem on the riverbank.

Something is standing in the shallows. Grey. Thin. Watching the water.

A wolf.

What is a wolf doing in a river full of grizzly bears?

Fishing.

The wolves of Katmai have learned the salmon run too. They wade in, pin fish with their paws, and carry them off. And when they cannot catch their own, they steal from the bears.

The wolf grabs a salmon from the shallows.

And Tula's cubs are standing ten meters away.

Is the wolf after the fish, or the cubs?

It looks at the cubs. Then it looks at Tula.

Tula drops her own fish and charges.

The wolf holds its ground for one long second, teeth bared, the salmon in its jaws.

Then it turns and runs into the trees. With the fish.

A wolf will not fight a grizzly mother for a cub. But it will steal her breakfast every chance it gets.

July is nearly over. And something is changing at the falls.

Fewer salmon are jumping. Then fewer still.

The run is ending. The bears are getting desperate.

Downstream, a young bear is floating in the deep water with its whole head under the surface. For ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty.

Is it drowning?

No. It is snorkeling.

With the jumping fish almost gone, the young bears search the river bottom for salmon that have already died. Its eyes are open underwater, scanning the stones.

It comes up with a dead fish in its jaws. Then dives again.

Another young bear sees the fish and charges through the water. The two of them go under together, wrestling, rolling, surfacing in a spray of water, both jaws on the same salmon.

It tears in half. Each one swims off with a piece.

When the easy food runs out, every bear on the river finds a new way to keep eating. Or it goes hungry.

And the biggest bears do not plan to go hungry.

Up on the lip, Scar is still fishing. But he is not alone anymore.

Ridge is back. His neck has healed. And he is bigger than he was.

What happens when the second fight comes?

This time, there is no stare.

Ridge charges up the rocks and crashes into Scar on the lip of the falls.

They rise up together. Jaws locked on each other's necks. Claws raking across faces.

Blood in the foam.

Scar has held this spot for years. He has never lost.

But he is older now. And Ridge is younger, and hungrier, and he has been thinking about this fight all summer.

Ridge drives forward. Scar's back paw slips on the wet rock.

And the king of the waterfall goes over the edge.

He crashes into the pool below. He comes up, staggering, bleeding from the face.

He looks up at the lip.

Ridge is standing on it.

Will Scar climb back up and fight again?

He stands in the pool for a long time. Then he turns and limps away downstream.

A king does not always die. Sometimes he just walks away.

The river has a new king. And the new king remembers a mother who bit him in the neck.

Ridge comes down off the lip. And he walks straight toward Tula's cubs.

Can she stop him a second time?

The cubs bolt for the spruce tree. Bo climbs. Fern climbs. Tula turns and faces the biggest bear on the river.

He rears up. She rears up. He is twice her weight.

She hits him anyway.

They crash into the shallows, biting, roaring. He knocks her down. She comes up roaring. He knocks her down again.

She comes up again.

Every time he turns toward the tree, she is there.

What does it take to stop a bear like that?

Not strength. She does not have it.

Just the one thing he does not have. A reason that matters more than food.

Ridge stops. Breathing hard. Bleeding from a new wound on his face.

He looks at the tree. He looks at Tula.

And he turns away, back toward the falls.

But something has happened while the two biggest bears on the river were fighting.

Look at the falls.

The lip is empty.

For the first time all summer, nobody is standing on it.

Will Tula dare?

She climbs the rocks. One paw. Then the other. The cubs scramble down from the tree and follow.

She steps onto the lip.

And a salmon jumps straight into her jaws.

Then another.

Then another.

She does not chase. She does not lunge. She stands there with her mouth open, and the river does the work.

How long before Ridge comes back?

Not long. Minutes. But minutes on the lip are worth hours in the riffles.

When Ridge returns, Tula steps aside without a fight. She has had her turn.

And it was enough.

September. The leaves on the mountains are turning red.

Tula is unrecognisable. Round. Heavy. Her coat thick and shining.

The cubs have doubled in size.

They climb the mountain together, to a slope where the snow will soon be deep. And Tula begins to dig.

So why does one spot at the top of a waterfall matter so much that bears will fight to the death for it?

Because on this river, the winner is not the strongest bear. It is the fattest one.

Every salmon a bear has to chase costs energy. Every salmon that jumps into its mouth costs almost nothing.

A bear on the lip can catch dozens of fish a day without taking a step. A bear in the riffles might catch a handful and burn most of what it gains.

In a season this short, the difference between the lip and the riffles is the difference between a bear that wakes up in spring and a bear that never does.

For a male, that ledge of rock is survival.

For a mother, it is survival times three.

That is why Scar fought for it. That is why Ridge took it. And that is why Tula stepped onto it the moment it was empty.

One ledge. Barely wide enough for one bear. And every bear on the river knows exactly what it is worth.

Katmai is protected now, and the salmon runs of this corner of Alaska are among the largest left anywhere on Earth.

But every bear here depends on those fish coming home. When the salmon fail, the bears fail with them.

Protect the rivers, and you protect the bears.

Perhaps the creator built the bears' whole year around a few weeks of salmon to show us something. That even the most powerful animals on Earth live or die by a river they cannot control.

Somewhere on a mountain above Katmai tonight, a mother bear lies curled in a den with two cubs pressed against her.

Outside, the first snow is falling.

And far below, the river is quiet. Waiting for next summer.
