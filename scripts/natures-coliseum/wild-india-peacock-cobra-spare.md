# WILD INDIA | The Peacock That Hunts Cobras

A cobra rises out of the grass. Hood spread wide. Hissing. One bite carries enough venom to kill a grown man.

Most animals turn and run. But the animal facing this cobra does not run.

It is not a mongoose. It is not an eagle.

It is a peacock.

The most beautiful bird in India is also one of its most fearless snake hunters. And to understand why a bird covered in jewels would go looking for the most feared snake in the country, you have to follow one peacock through the most dangerous season of the year.

This is the dry forest of Rajasthan, in north-western India, near the edge of Ranthambore. A land of thorn trees, rocky hills and pale grass, baked by the sun for eight months of the year.

And at the top of a tall tree, as the first light touches the hills, a peacock wakes up.

We will call him Neel. His neck shines a deep electric blue. Behind him hangs a train of feathers longer than his whole body, almost one and a half meters of green and bronze, covered in glowing eyes.

He spent the night up here for a reason. In this forest, leopards hunt in the dark, and a peacock sleeping on the ground would never see the morning. So every evening, Neel flies up to the highest branch he can reach. And every morning, he glides down.

He lands with a soft thud in the grass, folds his train behind him, and starts to walk.

A peacock looks like a bird built for show. But look closer.

His legs are covered in hard, overlapping scales, like armour. On the back of each leg is a sharp spur, a curved spike of bone and horn that he uses as a weapon. And his beak is short, hard and quick, made for snatching things off the ground faster than they can escape.

Because a peacock will eat almost anything. Seeds and grain. Flowers. Beetles, termites and crickets. Frogs and lizards. And, when it finds one, a snake.

Neel walks slowly through the grass, head down, eyes scanning every movement.

A lizard darts out from under a rock. He lunges. One snap of the beak, and it is gone.

But for now, the snakes are hidden. Deep in their burrows, under rocks and in termite mounds, sheltering from the heat. For most of the year, the forest belongs to the sun.

And then, in June, the sky turns black.

The monsoon arrives like a wall. Thunder rolls across the hills. And for the first time in eight months, rain hammers down on the dry forest.

Within days, the land turns green. Pools fill. Frogs appear by the thousand. And the whole forest comes alive.

And Neel begins to dance.

He finds an open patch of ground, turns to face the peahens feeding nearby, and raises his train.

Two hundred feathers rise into a shimmering fan, taller than he is. A wall of eyes, glowing green, blue and bronze in the grey monsoon light.

These feathers are not his tail. His real tail is short and plain, hidden underneath. The great fan is made of feathers that grow from his back, and he grows a new set every single year, just for this season.

And here is something strange. There is no blue paint in those feathers. No green either. If you could grind a peacock feather into powder, it would be brown. The colours come from tiny structures in the feathers that bend the light, like a soap bubble or the surface of a CD. As he turns, the colours shift and flash.

Then he does something you can barely hear. He shakes the whole train, fast, so the feathers rattle and shiver. That rattle sends out a low rumble, too deep for human ears. But a peahen can feel it. Scientists believe the little crest of feathers on her head picks up the vibration, like an antenna.

One of the peahens stops feeding and walks closer. We will call her Gauri. Brown and grey, with a green neck and a crest of her own. She is not dazzling. She does not need to be. She is the one who chooses.

She walks slowly in front of him. Studying the feathers. The eyes. The way he shakes.

Neel turns his back to her. Then spins around, so the full fan of eyes fills her view, and shivers it again.

And Gauri stays.

Darwin himself once wrote that the sight of a peacock's feather made him sick. He could not understand why any animal would grow something so heavy and so obvious to predators. The answer was standing in front of him. The peahen. For a peacock, being chosen matters more than being safe.

But the monsoon does not only wake up the frogs and the peacocks.

The rain floods the burrows. And out of every crack and hole in the forest, the snakes come out.

The first one Neel finds is young. A cobra, barely as long as his train, moving through the wet grass after a frog.

Even a young cobra is dangerous. Its venom is already deadly. But it is small, and it is slow in the cool morning air.

Neel sees it and does not hesitate. He runs at it, wings half open.

The young cobra rears and spreads its hood. It strikes. But Neel is already in the air, leaping straight up, and the strike passes under his feet.

He lands behind it, and his beak hammers down on the back of its head. Once. Twice. The snake twists and lashes. He leaps back, then strikes again.

In less than a minute, it is over. Neel picks up the snake in his beak, shakes it hard, and swallows it whole.

Why would a peacock hunt something that could kill it? Part of the answer is food. A snake is a meal full of protein, right at the start of the breeding season, when a peacock needs every bit of energy he can get.

But the bigger reason is about something far more precious than a meal.

Peafowl nest on the ground. And on the ground, the most dangerous animal of all, for eggs and chicks, is a snake.

A few weeks later, Gauri disappears.

She has found a hollow in the thick grass under a thorn bush, a shallow scrape lined with leaves, hidden from above and from every side. And in it, she has laid four pale cream eggs.

For almost a month, she will sit on them, day and night, leaving only for a few minutes to eat and drink. Peacocks do not help. Neel stays away, still displaying to other females, still patrolling the forest.

Gauri is alone. And she is not the only animal looking at those eggs.

A jackal trots past, nose to the ground, and she flattens herself into the grass until it moves on. A monitor lizard flicks its tongue a few meters away. She hisses and spreads her wings, and it turns away.

But one evening, something much worse comes through the grass.

An Indian cobra. Not young this time. Nearly two meters long, thick and heavy, its scales glistening from the rain. On the back of its hood is a mark like a pair of spectacles.

Cobras eat rats, frogs and toads. But they also eat eggs. And this one has smelled the nest.

Gauri sees it coming. She rises off the eggs and spreads her wings, hissing, feathers puffed up to make herself as big as she can.

The cobra rears up to meet her. Its hood spreads wide, and it sways, as tall as she is.

For a moment, they face each other over the eggs.

Then Gauri does the one thing she can. She screams.

A peafowl's alarm call is one of the loudest sounds in the forest. A harsh, piercing cry that carries across the hills. And it carries to Neel.

He comes running through the grass, train held low behind him, faster than you would think a bird that size could move.

The cobra turns toward the new threat. And now it faces two birds.

Neel does not attack straight on. He circles. Wings half open, feathers raised, moving left, then right, never still. The cobra sways with him, hood flared, trying to keep its head pointed at him.

It strikes.

Neel jumps. Straight up, a meter into the air, wings beating, and the cobra's fangs close on nothing.

He comes down to one side and stabs at its head with his beak. The cobra pulls back, then strikes again. Again, he is in the air before it lands.

Gauri flaps and hisses from the other side. The cobra cannot watch both of them at once. It swings its head between them, hood swaying, hissing louder.

Neel strikes it on the back of the hood. The cobra recoils. And for the first time, it lowers its head.

It turns. And slides away into the grass, toward a burrow under the rocks.

Neel stands with his wings spread, watching it go. Then he folds them, shakes out his feathers, and walks away. Gauri settles back onto her eggs.

All four are still there.

How does a peacock survive a cobra? As far as scientists know, it is not immune to the venom. One clean bite could kill it.

Instead, it relies on speed. A cobra strikes forward, at the height of its raised head. A peacock escapes upward. It leaps and flaps straight into the air, so the strike passes beneath it. Its thick layers of feathers make it hard for fangs to reach the skin, and its legs are covered in hard scales.

And it never gives the snake a still target. Circling, dodging, leaping, striking at the head from the side, it turns the cobra's own deadly lunge into a weakness. Every missed strike leaves the snake stretched out and open.

Twenty-eight days after the first egg was laid, the shells begin to crack.

One by one, four peachicks push their way out. Soft, fluffy, brown and cream, with tiny wing feathers already showing.

Within a day, they are up and following their mother through the grass. Within a week or two, their wings are strong enough to flutter up into low branches.

Gauri teaches them everything. How to pick up seeds. How to catch insects. How to freeze when her alarm call rings out. And every evening, how to climb higher, branch by branch, until they can sleep safely off the ground.

Because the dangers have not gone away.

One night, as the family roosts in a tall tree, something moves on the ground below.

A leopard. Padding silently along a game trail, eyes glowing.

The peachicks never see it. But Neel does, from his branch high above. And he screams.

Again and again, his alarm call cuts through the night. And all across the forest, other animals hear it. Spotted deer lift their heads and stamp their feet. Langur monkeys bark from the treetops.

The leopard stops. Looks up. It knows it has been seen. In this forest, a hunter that has been spotted has already lost.

It slinks away into the dark. And the forest falls quiet again.

Peafowl are among the best lookouts in the Indian forest. From their high perches, they see leopards and tigers long before most other animals do. And when they call, the whole forest listens.

In the weeks that follow, the peachicks grow fast. Their legs get longer. Their wings get stronger. The young males will not grow their great trains for almost three years. But they already have the same sharp eyes, the same quick beaks, and the same fearlessness.

One morning, the eldest chick finds its first snake. A small one, hiding under a leaf.

It freezes. Then it does exactly what it has watched its father do. It circles. It jumps. It strikes at the head.

And when it is over, it swallows the snake whole.

By the end of the monsoon, the forest is green and full of life. And Neel does something surprising.

He drops his train.

One by one, the great feathers fall out and are left in the grass, eyes and all. After all that effort, after all those dances, he simply lets them go. For the rest of the year, he will look plain, almost ordinary. Then, before the next monsoon, he will grow a whole new set from scratch.

The cobra is still out there. Somewhere in the rocks, waiting for next year's rains, next year's eggs, next year's chicks.

And the peacocks will be waiting too.

This is the strange partnership of the Indian forest. The cobra hunts the peacock's eggs. The peacock hunts the cobra's young. Each keeps the other in check, and the forest stays in balance.

In India, the peacock is the national bird, and for thousands of years, people here have welcomed it near their homes and fields. Not only for its beauty, but because wherever peacocks walk, the snakes keep their distance.

Today, peafowl are protected by law across India. But they still face poaching for their feathers, and poisoning from pesticides used on farmland.

Perhaps the creator gave the peacock its beauty to be admired, and its courage to be respected. A bird that dances in the rain, and then steps out to face the deadliest snake in the forest.

Tonight, high in a tree at the edge of Ranthambore, a peacock sleeps with four young birds beside him.

And somewhere below, in the dark, a cobra stays very still in its burrow. Waiting for the rain.
