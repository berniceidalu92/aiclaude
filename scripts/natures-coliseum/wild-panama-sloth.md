# Why Sloths Risk Death Just to Poop

Thirty meters up, in the canopy of a rainforest, an animal is hanging upside down. It has barely moved all week.

Up here, it is almost invisible. Almost untouchable.

But today, it is going to let go of all that safety. It is going to climb down the tree, all the way to the forest floor, the most dangerous place in its world. Where jaguars hunt. Where it can barely walk.

And it is doing it for one reason.

To go to the toilet.

This is the story of the slowest mammal on Earth, and the strangest risk in the animal kingdom. And by the end, you will see that this tiny, dangerous journey may be one of the cleverest things in the whole rainforest.

This is the rainforest of Panama, in Central America. A world of giant trees, hanging vines and constant rain. Most of the life here never touches the ground. It lives up in the canopy, in a green roof of leaves stretching unbroken for kilometres.

And hanging from a branch of a cecropia tree is a brown-throated three-toed sloth.

We will call her Luna. She is about the size of a small dog, with a round face, a dark band across her eyes like a mask, and a permanent, gentle smile. Clinging to her chest is her baby, just a few weeks old.

Luna lives her whole life in slow motion. And there is a reason for that.

She eats leaves. Almost nothing but leaves. And leaves are one of the worst foods in the forest. They are tough, low in energy, and many are full of chemicals that make them hard to digest.

So Luna's body has made a deal. It runs on as little energy as possible.

Her stomach has several chambers, full of bacteria that slowly break the leaves down. A single meal can take weeks to pass through her body. Her muscles are small. Her body temperature rises and falls with the weather, like a lizard's. And she moves only when she has to, and never faster than she must.

Her slowness is not laziness. It is a survival strategy. An animal that eats almost nothing cannot afford to waste a single movement.

And her slowness hides another secret.

Look closely at her fur. It is not just brown. It is tinged with green.

Luna moves so little that algae grows in her coat. Each hair has tiny cracks that soak up rainwater, and in those cracks, a whole garden grows. Green algae. Fungi. Insects. Even tiny moths that live their whole lives in her fur.

Her coat grows the opposite way to most mammals, from her belly toward her back, so that when she hangs upside down, the rain runs straight off.

And the green algae does something else. It makes her look like a clump of leaves.

Because up in the canopy, there is a hunter that can see almost anything.

A harpy eagle. One of the most powerful eagles on Earth, with talons as long as a bear's claws. It hunts by sitting perfectly still for hours on a high branch, watching for the smallest movement in the trees.

And sloths are one of its favourite meals.

The eagle lands on a branch across the clearing. Its feathered crest rises. Its eyes sweep the canopy, branch by branch.

Luna sees it. And she does the only thing a sloth can do.

Nothing.

She does not run. She cannot. She simply stops. Head tucked in, arms still, her baby pressed flat against her chest. A greenish-brown lump among the leaves.

The eagle's gaze passes over her. Stops. Moves on.

For nearly an hour, neither of them moves.

Then the eagle spreads its huge wings, drops off the branch, and glides away through the trees.

Luna lifts her head. It turns slowly, further than you would think possible. A three-toed sloth has extra bones in its neck, and can turn its head almost three quarters of the way around, without moving its body at all.

This is how a sloth survives in the canopy. Not by speed. By stillness. A harpy eagle hunts movement. And a sloth barely moves.

So why would an animal that survives by staying still, high up, hidden in the leaves, climb all the way down to the ground?

About once a week, Luna does exactly that.

It begins with a change in her behaviour. For days she has been feeding and resting high in the crown of her tree. But this morning, she starts to climb down.

Head first. One arm. Then the other. Her long, curved claws hooking around the trunk.

It is a long way down. Thirty meters. And at sloth speed, the journey can take half an hour or more.

Every meter lower takes her further from the safety of the canopy, and deeper into the world of the hunters.

Down here, the forest is dark and still. Hardly any light reaches the floor. And somewhere in that darkness, a jaguar is walking.

Luna reaches the bottom of the trunk.

On the ground, a sloth is almost helpless. Her long arms and hooked claws are built for hanging, not walking. She cannot stand up on her legs. She drags herself forward on her belly, pulling with her arms.

She finds a spot at the base of her tree. And with her stubby tail, she digs a small hole in the leaf litter.

And there, at the bottom of the most dangerous journey of her week, she does what she came to do.

It is no small thing. She has been holding it for days, and a sloth can lose a remarkable amount of her body weight in a single visit.

Then she covers the hole with leaves, turns, and starts to climb back up.

But this time, she is not alone.

A few meters away, a shape moves between the trees. Golden fur. Black rosettes. A jaguar, nose down, following a scent trail across the forest floor.

Luna freezes against the trunk.

The jaguar is close now. Close enough that she could reach out and touch it. It stops. Lifts its head. Sniffs the air.

Luna does not move. Not a claw. Not a hair. Her baby clings motionless to her chest.

The jaguar's head turns toward the tree.

And then, a parrot shrieks somewhere in the canopy. The jaguar's ears flick toward the sound. It turns away, and pads off into the shadows.

Luna waits. A full minute. Two. Then, very slowly, she starts to climb again. Arm over arm, up and up, until she is back in the leaves, thirty meters above the forest floor.

She has survived another week.

Scientists believe a large share of all sloth deaths may happen during these trips to the ground. It is the moment they are most exposed, and least able to escape.

So why do it at all?

Other animals in the canopy, monkeys, birds, even other kinds of sloth, simply let their droppings fall from the trees. Luna could do the same. It would be quicker, easier, and far safer.

For a long time, nobody could explain it. And scientists are still not completely sure. But there is one answer that is stranger than anything you might expect.

It starts with the moths.

The tiny moths that live in Luna's fur cannot breed there. When Luna climbs down and leaves her droppings at the foot of the tree, the female moths fly down and lay their eggs in it. The caterpillars grow up inside her dung, eating it.

And when they become moths, they fly up into the canopy, find a sloth, and move into its fur.

But here is the twist. Scientists studying sloths found that the more moths a sloth carries, the more nutrients there are in its fur, and the more algae grows there. The moths seem to act like fertiliser for the garden on its back.

And some scientists think sloths may lick and eat that algae, which is rich in fat. For an animal living on a diet of tough leaves, it could be a valuable extra meal.

So by climbing down to the ground, Luna may be keeping her moths alive. The moths feed her algae. And the algae may feed her.

A garden that grows on her own back, kept alive by a weekly trip to the toilet.

There are other ideas too. Some scientists think the visits are a way for sloths to leave scent messages for each other, especially for finding a mate. Others think a sloth may be feeding her own favourite tree, returning nutrients to its roots.

The truth may be a mix of all three. But whatever the reason, it is important enough that a sloth will risk its life for it, every single week.

And sloths have one more surprise.

When the rains are heavy, the rivers burst their banks and the forest floods. Some of Luna's favourite trees stand across a stretch of water.

On land, a sloth is one of the slowest animals on Earth. But in water, it is a different animal.

Luna drops from a branch into the river. And she swims.

Arms reaching out in long, smooth strokes. Her body buoyant, floating easily, her head held above the surface. She moves through the water around three times faster than she can move on land.

A sloth can even slow its heart and hold its breath for a remarkably long time underwater. Far longer than most land mammals.

She reaches the far bank, hooks a claw into a hanging vine, and hauls herself back up into the trees, dripping and green.

The same animal that drags itself helplessly across the forest floor glides through the river like a natural swimmer.

Through all of this, her baby has never let go.

For the first months of its life, a baby sloth spends almost every moment clinging to its mother's chest. It feeds on her milk. And slowly, it starts to taste the leaves she eats, learning which trees are safe and which are not.

This matters more than you might think. Every sloth learns its diet from its mother. The leaves she eats become the leaves it will eat for the rest of its life.

As it grows, the baby starts to let go. A few centimetres at first. Then a whole branch. It hangs by itself, swaying, while Luna feeds nearby.

And one day, the young sloth will make its own first journey down a tree. Alone. Head first. All the way to the ground.

Because one day, it will have moths of its own to look after.

Today, sloths face dangers far worse than jaguars.

Their forests are being cut down for farms and roads. A sloth that cannot travel from tree to tree through the canopy is forced to cross the ground more often, where it meets cars, dogs and power lines. In parts of Central America, rescue centres now care for hundreds of injured and orphaned sloths every year.

But where the forest stands unbroken, sloths can still live the way they always have. Slowly. Quietly. High above the ground.

Perhaps the creator made the sloth to show us that the slowest life can still be full of surprises. That even the smallest, strangest habit can hold a whole hidden world.

Tonight, high in the canopy of a Panamanian rainforest, a sloth hangs asleep with her baby on her chest.

Below her, on the dark forest floor, a jaguar walks past the foot of her tree.

In a week, she will have to go down there again.
