# WILD RUAHA | The Lions That Hunt Giraffes

Eight lions on one giraffe. Five meters of bone and hooves at full gallop, and a lioness clinging to its neck.

One kick. A young lioness flies through the air like a rag doll, slams into the sand, and does not get up.

A two-hundred-kilo lion takes a hoof to the skull and drops where he stands. The hyenas are already running in.

Thirty hyenas pour out of the dark, screaming, onto a pride that has nothing left to fight with.

A lioness drinking at the last pool in the river. The water behind her explodes, and jaws close where she stood.

A mother giraffe kicking in every direction over her calf, as a lioness dives beneath her hooves. Nobody here is safe.

Eight lions hidden in the sand. A giant giraffe spreads its legs to drink. And the riverbed explodes.

It bolts. Eight lions sprint across the riverbed after it, fanning out left and right, cutting off every way back to firm ground.

The giraffe runs, but the sand swallows its hooves. A young lioness slips in low, behind the legs that can kill her.

One kick. It misses, and the hoof sinks into the sand. A scarred lioness leaps onto its back.

Three lions on its back. Two on its flanks. And the tallest animal on Earth is still standing.

Its neck swings like a hammer, and a lioness flies into the dust. Then a two-hundred-kilo male launches at its shoulders.

Eight lions. One giraffe. Only one side walks away. To find out who, we have to go back to the beginning.

Dawn. A small herd of giraffes is feeding along the riverbed.

Zuri does not pick the biggest. She picks the one at the edge, feeding alone, farthest from the others and closest to the riverbed.

Three lionesses crawl toward it through the grass, bellies to the ground. Then they run.

Sixty meters. Forty. The giraffe sees them and wheels away, five meters of legs eating up the ground.

The lionesses split. One wide on each side. One straight behind. Twenty meters. Ten.

The first one leaps for its back and slides off. The second goes for its flank and misses.

The third is young. She goes for the legs.

The giraffe kicks backwards without even looking.

One kick from a giraffe can kill a lion outright. The hoof catches her in the ribs, and she flies. Two meters through the air. She hits the sand, rolls, and lies still.

Three lions. One giraffe. And the giraffe wins.

Why would lions even try? Because it is the end of the dry season. The river has stopped flowing, and the zebra and antelope have scattered across a thousand square kilometres of dying bush. But one giraffe is more than a thousand kilos of meat, enough to feed a pride for a week.

And why go for the legs, not the throat? Because the throat is five meters up, out of reach of any lion on Earth. The only way to kill a giraffe is to make it fall. And the only way to make it fall is through the legs that can kill you.

On the sand, the young lioness lifts her head. She is alive. We will call her Kesi.

An older lioness with a scarred face and a torn lip walks back and lies down beside her. Zuri, the leader of the hunt. The whole pride waits. Why wait for a lioness who cannot hunt? Because against a giraffe, every single lion counts.

How do you bring down an animal that kicks in every direction, and stands taller than a house?

Wild Ruaha. The lions that hunt giraffes.

The giraffe that kicked Kesi does not get far.

It gallops straight into the ground of a much bigger bull. And he does not share it.

He sees a stranger on his ground and comes straight at him, at a gallop, neck already swinging.

He is enormous. Nearly six meters tall, his neck scarred from a hundred fights. We will call him the Tower.

The two bulls stand neck to neck, and swing. Giraffe bulls settle who rules this way. They call it necking. A two-meter neck swung that hard hits like a hammer, and a fight can go on until one bull can barely stand.

Heads like sledgehammers, thirty kilos of skull on the end of a two-meter neck. Crack. A blow to the ribs. Crack. A blow to the spine.

The smaller bull swings back and catches the Tower across the shoulder. The Tower barely moves.

Then he drops his head low, and swings from the ground up.

The smaller bull goes down on one knee. He scrambles up, and runs.

And the lions have been watching all of it. Because a beaten bull is a tired bull. And a tired bull kicks slower.

Four lionesses come out of the grass after the beaten bull. They pick him because he is limping, and breathing hard, and alone.

He runs. Across the riverbed, up the bank, out onto the open plain. A giraffe can gallop at fifty kilometres an hour. A lioness is faster, but only for a few hundred meters.

Two of them swing wide to cut him off. He turns, and runs straight into the other two.

They hit him on open ground. Two on his flanks. One on his back. Zuri at his legs.

He kicks. Zuri ducks, and the hoof whistles over her head.

He spins, and the lioness on his flank is flung into a thorn bush.

He drags the lioness on his back fifty meters through the dust, then rears, and she slides off his shoulders into the dirt.

And he is gone.

Four lions. One tired giraffe. And still, the giraffe wins.

Why? Look at the ground they fought on. Hard. Flat. Firm. On ground like this, a giraffe can plant its feet, kick, and run. It cannot fall. Zuri has learned something.

The chase has dragged the pride far out onto the open plain. And as night falls, a herd of zebra comes in to graze.

The lionesses circle downwind and watch. Not the fastest zebra. The old stallion limping at the back.

Two lionesses drive him out of the herd at full gallop, straight toward two more waiting in the dark. It goes down in seconds.

But a kill makes noise. And the noise carries.

A kilometre away, a hyena lifts its head. It whoops. And from every direction, the clan comes running, following the sound across the dark plain.

They have eaten for less than a minute when the laughing starts.

Hyenas. One. Five. Fifteen. Thirty. In Ruaha, they are not just scavengers. They are the lions' biggest rivals.

They pour out of the dark, screaming, circling the kill.

Zuri charges. A hyena bites her flank. She spins and swats it into the sand. Three more rush in behind her.

Another lioness is surrounded. Four hyenas at once, snapping at her legs. She roars, slashing, backing toward the others.

Four lionesses against thirty hyenas. The pride backs away. The hyenas take the zebra.

Why give up a kill? Because a hyena bite to a leg can cripple a lioness. And in a drought, a crippled lioness is a dead lioness. Without a male, four lionesses cannot hold a kill against a clan this size.

Then, out of the dark, a roar that shakes the riverbed. He heard the fight from the far side of the territory, and he has been running for two kilometres.

A male lion comes in at full speed. Black mane. Two hundred kilos. He hits the hyenas like a falling tree.

He pins one under his paws. He swats another into the air. They scatter, screaming. One does not get up.

This is Jabari. The pride male. Half as heavy again as any lioness. And before this drought is over, that weight will matter more than anything.

The fight leaves the pride with nothing but thirst. And in this drought, there is only one place left to drink.

The last pool in the river. A green-brown puddle in a kilometre of sand. In the dry season, every crocodile in the river is packed into pools like this. But it is the only water for twenty kilometres. Every drink is a gamble.

Zuri lowers her head to the water.

The crocodile has been watching the bank since dawn. It saw her coming down to drink. It sank, and glided toward her along the bottom without a single ripple.

The water behind her explodes.

A Nile crocodile, four meters long, lunges out of the shallows. Its jaws snap shut a hand's width from her face.

She leaps back, snarling. The crocodile slides back under, and the whole pool ripples.

At the far end, a giraffe lifts its head at the splash. Then lowers it again.

The Tower. Front legs spread wide. Head down at the water.

A giraffe's neck is too short to reach the ground while its legs stand straight. So to drink, it must spread them wide. For those few seconds, it cannot run, and it cannot kick properly. It is the most dangerous moment of a giraffe's day.

Zuri picks her target. Not the giraffes at the other pools. The Tower, drinking alone, legs spread, while the giraffe on watch looks the other way.

Seven lions creep along the riverbed. More lions than the pride has ever used on one giraffe. Kesi limps at the back. Twenty meters. Ten.

The pride charges.

But the Tower's head is already up.

How did he know? Look at the top of the bank. Another giraffe, standing perfectly still. Giraffes drink in turns. While one drinks, another keeps watch. And from six meters up, a watcher sees everything.

He bolts up the riverbed with seven lions behind him, sand flying from every stride. For two hundred meters they gain on him.

Then the bank. He takes it in three strides, out onto hard ground, and spins to face them. Back on firm earth, where a giraffe can fight.

Jabari leaps for his flank. And the Tower kicks.

One front hoof, as big as a dinner plate, straight forward, faster than a lion can move. It catches Jabari on the side of the head.

He drops where he stands. And he does not move.

Is he dead?

The hyenas have been following the pride since the zebra. Now they have seen the biggest lion go down. And they go straight for the weakest point in the pride: the lion on the ground.

Fifteen of them, running in from three sides.

The lionesses close around Jabari. Kesi, ribs still cracked, steps in front of him and roars.

A hyena lunges for Jabari's leg. Zuri catches it by the neck and throws it. Another goes for Kesi. She swats it across the face, and it backs off yelping.

Two more hit Zuri from behind. She rolls, comes up roaring, and they scatter.

Behind them, Jabari moves. He lifts his head. Gets one paw under himself. Then another.

He staggers up, blood on his jaw, and roars. The hyenas scatter into the dark.

He is alive. But his jaw is swollen, and a tooth is broken. He cannot eat. And neither can the pride.

Hunger drives them at the first giraffe they see. A mother with a young calf. They are not after the mother. They are after the calf, smaller, slower, easier. All they have to do is split it from her. The calf is the size of a lion. Its mother is five times that.

She sees them coming at a hundred meters. She could outrun them. Her calf cannot.

She stops, plants herself over the calf, and kicks. Front legs. Back legs. In every direction at once.

A lioness takes a hoof to the shoulder and rolls away. Zuri dives under the mother's belly for the calf, and a back hoof slams down beside her head, so close it throws sand in her eyes.

Another lioness goes for the calf from the side. The mother spins on the spot, and kicks her clean off her feet.

The pride pulls back. Mother and calf walk away.

Why didn't she run and save herself? Because the safest place for a calf is directly underneath its mother. Any lion that wants it has to go through four legs that can each kill.

One failure too many. The youngest male breaks away from the pride, and goes alone. Young males often try hunts like this to prove themselves, and many never get the chance to try again.

At midday, he picks his target. Not a calf. A full-grown giraffe, to prove himself. He stalks it through the heat haze, then he charges.

One lion. One giraffe.

It sees him coming and runs. He chases it across the burning sand, closing, closing, until he is right under its tail.

He leaps and sinks his claws into its hindquarters, hanging on as it bolts.

The giraffe does not even try to kick him off. It just runs. To an animal that size, one lion is just extra weight. One lion cannot even slow a giraffe down.

His grip fails. He drops. Rolls. And a back hoof slams down where he landed a heartbeat earlier.

He limps back toward the rocks where the pride's cubs are hidden, bleeding from a cut on his leg.

And he leaves a trail.

That night, the hyenas follow it, noses to the ground, at a steady lope, straight to the rocks. A hyena can follow the scent of blood for kilometres. And hyenas will kill lion cubs whenever they get the chance.

Zuri hears the laughing from half a kilometre away, and she runs.

A hyena is already pushing its head into the cave where her three cubs are hiding. Zuri hits it from behind. They roll down the rocks together, biting, screaming.

Two more hyenas come at her. Then four. One gets a grip on her back leg.

And then Jabari is there, swollen jaw and all. He crashes into the clan like he did on the first night.

When the dust settles, the hyenas are gone. All three cubs are still in the cave.

But the pride cannot survive another night like this. Tomorrow, it needs the biggest kill there is.

The Tower.

Zuri has chosen her target: the Tower, and only the Tower. She has chosen her ground: the soft sand. And she has chosen her moment: when he drinks.

The next evening, he walks out onto the soft sand of the riverbed to reach the last pool.

He does not see the lions. They are lying flat in the sand, all around the pool. Eight of them. Every lion the pride has.

He spreads his front legs to drink.

And Zuri rises out of the sand.

The pride explodes from three sides at once.

The Tower runs for the bank and the hard ground beyond it. But the lions on that side are already there, cutting him off.

He swings away, back across the open sand, eight lions closing from every side. His hooves sink with every stride.

Kesi reaches him first. The same young lioness who was kicked into the dust at the very start.

She goes low, behind his back legs, out of reach of his kick, and bites into the tendon above his hoof.

The Tower kicks. And misses. His hoof sinks into the sand.

Zuri leaps onto his back. Two more lionesses hit his flanks.

Three lions on his back. And the Tower is still standing.

He swings his neck like a hammer, and a lioness is thrown into the sand. He turns, and comes for the rest.

The young male leaps for his flank and is kicked away. He gets up, and leaps again.

Then Jabari hits him. Two hundred kilos of lion, landing high on his shoulders, dragging down with everything he has.

The Tower's front legs buckle in the sand. He sways. He fights. He tries to plant his feet.

There is nothing to plant them on.

And the tallest animal on Earth comes down.

So how did eight lions kill an animal that should have killed them?

Look at the final hunt again.

They waited until the river turned to sand. They hid in it. They struck at the one moment he could not run, when he spread his legs to drink.

They cut him off from the bank, so he could never reach the hard ground where he had escaped them before. Every kick sank. Every stride sank.

And then, the weight. Lionesses can hold a giraffe. It takes a male's weight on its shoulders to pull it down. That is why the pride needed Jabari.

A giraffe's greatest weapon is its height. Once the ground gives way, its height is the reason it cannot get back up.

One lion could not kill a giraffe. Three could not. Four could not. Seven could not.

It took all eight. And a drought that turned the river into sand.

For the first time in weeks, the pride eats until it can eat no more.

Up in the rocks, Zuri's three cubs are fed. Jabari's jaw is healing. And Kesi lies in the sand beside the kill, the young lioness who started this story flying through the air, and finished it holding the Tower's legs.

Ruaha is one of the last great strongholds for lions in Africa, home to one of the largest lion populations left on the continent.

But the Great Ruaha River is in trouble. Upstream, water is taken for farms and towns, and in the dry season the river that once flowed all year now runs dry for months.

When the river dies, everything that depends on it is pushed to the edge. Even lions.

Perhaps the creator built the giraffe tall enough to see every danger coming, and the lion patient enough to wait for the one moment it cannot, to show us something. That in the wild, strength alone never wins. Timing does. And working together does.

Somewhere along the dry bed of the Great Ruaha tonight, eight lions lie together in the sand, full for the first time in weeks.

The river is still waiting for the rain.

And so are they.
