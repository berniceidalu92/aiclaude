# WILD KOMODO | The Dragon That Bites Once and Waits

One bite. That is all he needs.

A water buffalo seven times his weight stands at the edge of a mud hole. She has horns that could open him like a knife. She has a herd that will charge anything that comes near.

And he walks straight toward her.

He strikes once, tearing into her leg. Then he lets go and steps back. He does not chase. He does not fight.

He waits.

Because something has entered her blood. Something that will not let it stop flowing. And from that moment on, the most powerful animal on this island is already losing a race she does not even know she is running.

But he is not the only one who will smell her.

Somewhere on these hills, an even larger dragon is already tasting the air.

And high in the trees, a young dragon is hiding from all of them. Because on this island, the biggest danger to a baby dragon is a grown one.

Before this story is over, two giants will rise up on their hind legs and fight for the prize. And one small dragon will have to survive the feast.

So how does a hunter that cannot overpower its prey bring down an animal seven times its size?

This is the story of the last dragons on Earth. It is as though the creator kept one age of monsters alive, on a handful of islands, just so we could see it.

Wild Komodo. The dragon that bites once and waits.

These are the islands of Komodo and Rinca, in Indonesia.

Steep, sunburnt hills. Dry grass. Valleys of palms. Beaches where the forest runs straight into a turquoise sea.

They look like paradise. But on these islands, the largest predator is not a cat or a wolf or a bear.

It is a lizard.

The Komodo dragon is the biggest lizard alive. The largest males grow to three meters long, heavy enough to bring down a deer, a wild boar, even a buffalo.

Its ancestors walked the Earth millions of years ago. Today, its kind survives only here, and on a few neighbouring islands, and nowhere else in the world.

And on Rinca, one dragon rules the valley.

This is Naga.

He is the largest male for miles. Old scars cover his armoured skin. His claws are curved like hooks, and his mouth is lined with sixty serrated teeth that are shed and replaced throughout his life.

But it is not his teeth that make him deadly.

Watch his tongue.

It flicks in and out, tasting the air. Every flick gathers scent particles and carries them to an organ in the roof of his mouth. With it, Naga can pick up the smell of blood from kilometres away.

Right now, he is smelling buffalo.

Down in the valley, a herd of water buffalo gathers at a mud hole to escape the heat. Huge animals. Six hundred kilos of muscle and horn. Naga weighs barely a seventh of that.

He cannot chase them. A dragon can only sprint in short bursts.

He cannot wrestle them. One swing of those horns would break his spine.

So he does something no buffalo expects.

He lies down beside the game trail, flat against the dry grass, and he waits.

One hour. Two. Three.

He does not move. Flies land on his eyelids. The sun climbs higher. Still he waits.

Then the herd begins to move along the trail, toward fresh grazing.

A young female lingers at the back.

She passes within a few meters of the grass where Naga lies.

He explodes forward.

In a single lunge, he clamps onto her hind leg and pulls back, his serrated teeth slicing deep. The buffalo bellows and kicks, smashing him into the dirt. The herd wheels around. The lead bull lowers his horns and charges.

Naga lets go.

He scrambles backward into the brush as the bull thunders past, horns slashing at empty air.

The female limps after her herd. There is blood running down her leg. But she is walking. She is alive. And the herd closes around her like a wall.

To anyone watching, the dragon has failed.

He has not.

Because the bite is only the beginning.

For years, people believed a Komodo bite killed through bacteria in the dragon's mouth. The truth is stranger.

Glands in Naga's lower jaw produce venom. It stops blood from clotting. It lowers blood pressure. It sends the body into shock.

The wound will not close. It will keep bleeding. And with every hour, the buffalo will grow weaker.

So Naga does not need to win the fight. He only needs to follow.

He flicks his tongue, finds her scent among all the others, and begins to walk.

The buffalo herd moves to a waterhole. The wounded female wades deep into the water, standing in the cool mud, as if it could soothe the burning in her leg.

The herd gathers around her.

On the bank, Naga stops and lies down again. Watching. Waiting.

But he is not the only one watching.

Above the waterhole, high in the branches of a tamarind tree, a small shape clings to the bark.

A young Komodo dragon. Barely a year old. Speckled with bands of yellow and green. Small enough to fit along a single branch.

We will call her Kecil.

She lives in the trees for a reason.

On this island, adult dragons will eat their own young. For their first years of life, Komodo hatchlings hide in the trees, feeding on insects, geckos, and eggs, staying far above the reach of the giants below.

Kecil can smell the buffalo's blood too. She is hungry. She is curious.

And she is about to learn exactly why she should be afraid.

A medium-sized dragon has followed the scent to the waterhole. It is not as big as Naga. But it is many times larger than Kecil.

It stops beneath her tree. It lifts its head. Its tongue flicks toward the branch where she is hiding.

Then it begins to climb.

Young dragons can climb well. Older, heavier ones struggle. But this one is still light enough. Its claws dig into the bark. It pulls itself up, one heavy step at a time.

Kecil freezes. Then she runs.

She scrambles along the branch, out to where it thins and bends. The bigger dragon follows. The branch sags beneath its weight.

Kecil leaps.

She drops into the next tree, catches a branch with her claws, and keeps climbing, higher and higher, until she is lost among the leaves.

The bigger dragon stops. It looks up. Then it slowly backs down the trunk.

She has survived. For today.

As darkness falls, the waterhole fills with eyes.

Three smaller dragons have followed the scent of blood from across the valley. They lie on the far bank, spaced apart, motionless, like logs washed up by a flood. None of them is bold enough to challenge the herd. None of them is willing to leave.

In the middle of the water, the lead bull stands guard over the wounded female all night long, horns lowered, snorting at every ripple.

Nobody on this island sleeps tonight.

The next morning, the wounded buffalo is still on her feet.

But something has changed.

Her head hangs lower. She falls behind the herd more often. When she stops to drink, she stands for a long time, as though she has forgotten why she came.

The venom is working.

And Naga is never far behind.

He shadows the herd through the valley, never closing in, never letting them out of his range. When they rest, he rests. When they move, he moves.

High above, Kecil is hunting too.

She creeps along a branch toward a gecko pressed against the bark. She freezes. She waits, exactly as the giants below her do. Then she snaps it up in a single bite.

Patience is the first lesson every dragon learns. Even the smallest ones.

Twice, the lead bull turns and charges him. Twice, Naga slips away into the grass and returns as soon as the bull has gone.

He does not need to hurry. Time is on his side.

But time is also carrying the scent of blood across the island.

On the hills above the valley, another dragon lifts its head.

He is even larger than Naga. A massive, battle-scarred male, heavier, longer, older. His jaw is crooked from an old fight. His tail is notched where something once tried to bite it off.

We will call him Raja.

He flicks his tongue. He tastes the blood on the wind.

And he starts down the hill.

By the second evening, the buffalo can barely walk.

She stumbles to the edge of the herd and stops. Her legs tremble. Her breath comes in heavy gasps.

The herd moves on without her.

Naga steps out of the grass.

For the first time in two days, he walks toward her in the open. Slowly. Patiently. His tongue flicking, tasting how weak she has become.

The buffalo sees him. She lowers her horns. She stamps the ground.

Then her legs fold, and she drops to her knees.

It looks like the end.

It is not.

Because as Naga closes the last few meters, the buffalo heaves herself back up. With the last of her strength, she swings her horns, and catches the dragon full in the side.

Naga is flung sideways into the dust.

He rolls, scrambles, and backs away, hissing. A long gash runs along his ribs.

The buffalo turns and staggers away, not toward her herd, but down the slope toward the sea.

And on the ridge above, Raja has arrived.

Two giant dragons. One wounded buffalo. And a long, cold night ahead.

Naga is wounded. Raja is bigger, fresher, and heading for the same prize.

Whatever happens now, only one of them will eat.

Night falls over Rinca.

The buffalo has reached the edge of a mangrove swamp, where the land breaks up into mud and twisting roots and channels of seawater. She wades in and stands there in the dark, as if the water could hide her.

Naga follows her scent down the slope.

When he reaches the channel, he does not hesitate. Komodo dragons are strong swimmers. They have been seen crossing the open sea between islands.

He slides into the black water and swims, his tail sweeping side to side, his head low on the surface.

The current pulls at him. The wound on his side stings in the salt. Somewhere in the darkness, something splashes, and he stops, head raised, tongue flicking, until the water is still again.

Then he keeps swimming.

Behind him, a much larger shape slips into the same channel.

Raja is following too.

And in the mangroves above, another small shape is moving through the branches. Kecil has followed the scent all the way from the waterhole, creeping from tree to tree in the dark.

Every dragon in the valley is now converging on one place.

Dawn.

The buffalo is lying on her side in the shallow mud between the mangrove roots.

This time, she does not get up.

Three days after a single bite, the most powerful animal on the island has fallen to a hunter a seventh of her size.

Naga drags himself out of the water and climbs onto the mud beside her. He has followed her for three days. He has been kicked, charged, and gored.

The prize is finally his.

And then a shadow falls across the mud.

Raja has arrived.

He climbs from the channel, water streaming from his enormous body, and walks straight toward the carcass.

Naga turns to face him.

What happens next is one of the most extraordinary sights in the animal kingdom.

The two dragons circle each other, hissing, tongues flicking.

Then, together, they rise up onto their hind legs.

Standing almost as tall as a man, using their tails for balance, they lock their front legs around each other and grapple, chest to chest, each one trying to throw the other to the ground.

They push. They twist. Claws rake across armoured hide. Their huge bodies sway in the morning light like two wrestlers in an ancient ritual.

Raja is heavier. He forces Naga backward, step by step, toward the water.

Naga's wounded side is bleeding again. His legs are shaking.

For a moment, it looks as if three days of patience are about to be stolen in a single fight.

But Naga has one advantage.

He knows this mud. He has been standing in it all night.

As Raja pushes forward, his back foot slides on the slick roots. He lurches. And Naga throws all his weight into that one instant.

The giant topples sideways and crashes into the mud.

He gets up slowly. He hisses. He looks at the carcass, and then at Naga, still standing.

And Raja turns and slides back into the channel.

The fight is over.

Naga has won.

But in the middle of the struggle, no one noticed a small shape creeping along a mangrove root toward the feast.

Kecil.

She is hungry, and the smell of the buffalo is overwhelming. She edges closer to the far side of the carcass, as far from Naga as she can get.

Naga lifts his head.

His tongue flicks toward her.

Kecil does the only thing she can. She rolls in the mud and filth at the edge of the swamp, coating her whole body with it.

Young dragons do this for a reason. The smell makes them unappealing, even to a hungry adult.

Naga watches her for a long moment.

Then he turns back to the buffalo and begins to feed.

Kecil darts in, tears away a small scrap, and scrambles back up into the mangrove roots, where she eats in safety, high above the giant below.

Naga eats for hours.

A Komodo dragon can swallow up to eighty percent of its own body weight in a single meal. Its jaws can open wide enough to swallow huge chunks whole. After a feast like this, it may not need to eat again for weeks.

When he finally stops, he climbs onto a sunny bank above the mangroves and lies still, letting the morning warmth spread through his body.

The wound on his side will heal. The scars will join the others.

So how does a hunter that cannot overpower its prey bring down an animal seven times its size?

Not by strength. The buffalo was always stronger.

Not by speed. He could never outrun her.

He wins with patience. One bite, and then the long walk behind his prey, for hours, for days, for as long as it takes.

The buffalo had horns, a herd, and seven times his weight. Naga had time.

And on this island, time is the most dangerous weapon of all.

But time is running out for the dragons themselves.

Only a few thousand Komodo dragons remain in the wild. They live on a handful of islands, and nowhere else on Earth.

As the climate warms and the seas rise, scientists warn that the low coastal lands these dragons depend on could shrink. Their islands are already small. There is nowhere else for them to go.

Komodo National Park now protects much of their home, and their numbers there have held steady. But their future depends on those islands staying wild.

Perhaps the creator left these dragons on just a few islands to remind us how rare and fragile the ancient world really is. One age of giants still walking in the sun.

Somewhere on Rinca tonight, Naga lies in the warm earth, full and still. High above him, in the mangroves, a young dragon sleeps safely in the branches, one day closer to growing up.

All they ask is an island to call their own.
