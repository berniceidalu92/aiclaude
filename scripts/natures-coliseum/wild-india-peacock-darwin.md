# WILD INDIA | The Bird That Made Darwin Sick

In the spring of 1860, Charles Darwin, the man who explained how life on Earth evolves, sat down and wrote a letter to a friend.

And in it, he confessed something strange.

"The sight of a feather in a peacock's tail," he wrote, "whenever I gaze at it, makes me sick."

Darwin had just shown the world that nature keeps what helps an animal survive, and throws away what does not. Fast legs. Sharp eyes. Good camouflage.

And then there was the peacock.

A bird dragging a train of feathers longer than its whole body. So bright it can be seen from across a forest. In a land full of tigers and leopards.

By Darwin's own rules, the peacock should not exist.

And yet, here it is. And to understand how, you have to follow one peacock through one year, in the forests where it has lived for millions of years.

This is Bandhavgarh, in central India. A land of sal forest, rocky hills and open meadows. And one of the places on Earth with the most tigers.

It is still dark. Somewhere below, in the long grass, a tiger is calling.

And high in a tall tree, a peacock is listening.

We will call him Raja. He is six years old, and in his prime. His neck shines a deep electric blue. And behind him hangs his train. Almost one and a half meters of green and bronze feathers, covered in glowing eyes.

He spent the night up here, as every peacock does. On the ground, in the dark, he would be easy prey. Up here, nothing can reach him.

As the sky turns pale, the tiger's calls fade into the distance. And Raja drops from his branch, spreads his wings, and glides down to the meadow below, his train streaming out behind him like a comet.

He lands, folds it, and starts to feed. Seeds. Insects. A lizard that moves too slowly.

And every few seconds, his head comes up. Watching the edge of the forest.

This is exactly what bothered Darwin. Every morning, Raja walks through a forest full of hunters, carrying the most obvious signal in the jungle. A tiger does not have to search for him. He is shouting where he is.

So why would nature build a bird like this?

For most of the year, the forest is dry and quiet, and the peacocks keep a low profile, feeding in small groups.

But in June, the monsoon arrives. Thunder rolls across the hills. The rain pours down, and within days the forest turns green.

And the peacocks change completely.

Every morning, the males gather at the same open patches of ground in the forest. Each one claims a small space of his own, only a few meters across, and defends it against every other male.

Raja's spot is a patch of bare earth under a mahua tree. He has held it for three years.

But this year, a younger male wants it.

They meet at the edge of the clearing, necks stretched, crests raised. They circle each other, slowly. Then the young male leaps.

He jumps into the air, feet first, kicking out with the sharp spurs on the back of his legs. Raja leaps to meet him. They collide in mid-air, wings beating, feathers flying, and fall back to the ground.

Again. Leaping, kicking, slashing with their spurs.

The young male lands badly. Raja jumps on him, driving him back across the clearing. And the young male turns, and runs.

The spot is Raja's. Now he needs someone to show it to.

A small group of peahens wanders into the clearing. Brown and grey, with green necks and small crests. Plain, compared to him.

But they are the ones who decide everything.

Raja turns to face them, and raises his train.

Two hundred feathers rise behind him into a shimmering fan, taller than he is. Green, blue, bronze and gold. And scattered across it, more than a hundred eyes, glowing in the grey monsoon light.

And this is where the peacock becomes far stranger than most people know.

First, this is not his tail. His real tail is short and dull, hidden underneath. The great fan is made of special feathers that grow from his lower back. And he grows a brand new set every single year, just for these few weeks.

Second, there is no blue in these feathers. No green either. If you ground a peacock feather into powder, it would be brown. The colours are made by tiny structures inside the feathers, so small they bend the light itself, like the shimmer on a soap bubble. That is why, as Raja turns, the colours flash and change.

And third, there is a part of this display you cannot hear.

Raja shakes his train. Fast. The feathers rattle and shiver. And that shiver sends out a deep rumble, far too low for human ears. But scientists have found that the little crest of feathers on a peahen's head vibrates at almost exactly the same rhythm as the shaking train. She may not just see his display. She may feel it.

One peahen steps forward. We will call her Meera.

She walks slowly in front of Raja, then around him. Studying him. He turns his back to her, then spins around, so the whole wall of eyes fills her view. And shivers it again.

For a long time, people thought peahens counted the eyes. And one famous study found that males with more eyes attracted more females.

But when scientists later put tiny cameras on peahens' heads to see exactly where they looked, they found something surprising. The peahens hardly ever looked at the top of the train at all. They looked at the bottom. The base of the feathers. The legs. The way he moved.

The great fan may be mostly an advertisement, seen from far away, to bring them in close. Once they are close, the inspection is about the details.

And there is one more secret.

Every so often, Raja gives a loud, sharp call. A hoot, then a rush of short notes. Peacocks normally make this call when they are mating. Other peahens hear it, and come to see what is going on.

But Raja is not mating. He is calling anyway.

Scientists studying peacocks in India found that males make this call even when no female is with them. And when they do, more females come to their display. It seems the peacock is not above a little lie.

Meera walks around him one last time. Then she crouches in front of him.

She has chosen.

And this was the answer Darwin found, eleven years after his letter.

In 1871, he published a new idea. Nature, he realised, does not only keep what helps an animal survive. It also keeps what helps an animal be chosen. If the peahens prefer the males with the most dazzling trains, then those males have the most chicks. And over thousands of generations, the trains grow more and more dazzling.

Darwin called it sexual selection. The peacock's train did not evolve to beat the tiger. It evolved to win the peahen.

But that only raises another question. Why would a peahen choose a male carrying something so heavy and so dangerous?

One answer is that the danger is the point.

A big, perfect train is hard to grow. It takes food, health and strength. A sick or weak male cannot build one, and could not survive carrying one. So when a peahen chooses the male with the finest train, she is choosing the male who has proved he is strong enough to survive in spite of it.

And it seems to work. In one experiment, scientists raised the chicks of different peacocks under exactly the same conditions. The chicks fathered by males with the most elaborate trains grew faster, and were more likely to survive.

When Meera chose Raja, she was not just choosing beauty. She was choosing the best father for her chicks.

But Darwin's other worry was still real. The train is a terrible thing to carry through a tiger forest.

Or is it?

A few weeks later, Raja is feeding at the edge of the meadow in the late afternoon, his train trailing behind him in the grass.

At the forest edge, something is watching him.

A tigress. Lying flat in the long grass, her stripes melting into the shadows. She has been creeping closer for twenty minutes. Thirty meters. Twenty.

Raja's head comes up. He has not seen her. But he has seen a langur in the trees above go suddenly still.

The tigress charges.

Raja does not run. He explodes upward. Two powerful beats of his wings, and he is off the ground. A third, and he is above the grass. The tigress leaps, paws outstretched, and her claws swipe through the trailing feathers of his train.

Three more wingbeats. And he is in the branches of a tree, ten meters up.

The tigress lands in the grass below, a single long feather caught in her claws. She looks up at him. Then turns, and walks back into the forest.

Raja screams. Again and again. And all across the meadow, spotted deer lift their heads and stamp their feet, and the langurs bark from the trees. The whole forest now knows where the tigress is.

Darwin was sure the train must slow the peacock down. But when scientists finally filmed peacocks taking off, with and without their full trains, they found almost no difference. The train is light, and it streams out behind the bird, out of the way of its wings. A healthy peacock can still burst into the air in a second.

The train is a burden. But not nearly as heavy as Darwin feared.

Meanwhile, Meera has disappeared.

She has made a nest on the ground, a shallow scrape hidden in thick grass under a bush. And in it, she has laid five pale eggs.

Raja will not help her. He stays at his display ground, dancing for other females. Everything else falls to Meera.

For four weeks she sits on the eggs, hidden from tigers, jackals, mongooses and snakes. Leaving them only for a few minutes each day to feed.

And then, one by one, the shells crack.

Five peachicks. Fluffy, brown and cream, with tiny wings already sprouting.

Within a day, they are following her through the grass. Within a couple of weeks, they can flutter up into low branches. And every evening, she leads them a little higher, until they can sleep safely in the trees, just like their father.

Two of the chicks are males. In three years, they will grow their first full trains. And because their father was Raja, they will have a good chance of growing fine ones.

By the end of the monsoon, the forest is lush and green. And Raja does something remarkable.

He drops his train.

One by one, the great feathers fall out and are left lying in the grass. Eyes and all. After all that effort, all those dances and fights, he simply lets them go.

For the rest of the year, he will look plain, almost ordinary. And then, before the next monsoon, he will grow the whole thing again from nothing.

The bird that made Darwin sick ended up teaching him one of the most important ideas in biology. That life is not only a struggle to survive. It is also a struggle to be chosen. And sometimes, being chosen matters so much that an animal will carry something dangerous, heavy and beautiful, just for the chance.

Today, the Indian peacock is the national bird of India, and it is protected by law across the country. In many villages, people welcome peafowl near their homes and fields, and the birds are doing well.

But they still face poaching, and poisoning from pesticides on farmland. And in some forests, they are now rarer than they once were.

Perhaps the creator made the peacock to remind us that not everything in nature is about survival. Some things are simply about beauty, and the courage to show it.

Tonight, in the forests of Bandhavgarh, a peacock sleeps high in a tree, his train already gone, the tiger calling somewhere below.

Next monsoon, he will be back on the same patch of earth under the mahua tree.

Dancing again.
