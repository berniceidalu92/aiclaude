# WILD KARAKORAM | Why the Ibex Doesn't Belong on the Ground

A ledge no wider than a hand, three hundred meters above the valley floor. And standing on it, perfectly still, is a goat.

She is not lost, and she is not trapped. She chose this ledge, because up here nothing can reach her.

Down on the flat ground below, there is grass, water and salt. Everything she needs to live.

And every time she goes down to get it, something is waiting to kill her.

This is the story of an animal that lives its whole life on the edge of a cliff. Not because it has to. Because the ground is the most dangerous place it could ever stand.

High in the Karakoram, in the far north of Pakistan, the mountains rise so steeply that most of the land is not land at all. It is walls. Granite and slate, cracked and folded, climbing toward peaks of seven and eight thousand meters.

This is the home of the Himalayan ibex. And this is one ibex family.

The mother we will call Dawa. She is eight years old, light and sandy-grey, with short curved horns and a body built like a coiled spring. Beside her is her daughter, born last summer, still learning the mountain.

And far above them, on a slope of his own, is the male. We will call him Kesang. More than twice Dawa's weight, with a dark beard and horns so long they sweep back over his shoulders like a pair of curved swords.

To understand why this family lives on the cliffs, you have to start with their feet.

An ibex hoof is not one solid block. It is split into two toes, and each toe can move on its own, spreading apart to grip the rock or squeezing together to fit into a crack.

The outer rim of each toe is hard and sharp, like the edge of a knife, to catch on the smallest ridge. But the center is soft and rubbery, and it moulds itself to the stone like the sole of a climbing shoe.

Behind the hoof, a pair of small dewclaws act as brakes. On a steep descent, the ibex digs them into the rock to stop itself sliding.

And then there is the balance. An ibex can stand with all four hooves together on a point of rock no bigger than a dinner plate. From a standstill, it can leap nearly two meters straight up a wall, and land on a ledge it can barely see.

In Italy, their cousins, the Alpine ibex, have been filmed climbing the almost vertical wall of a dam, just to lick the salt from the stone.

On a cliff, there is no animal on Earth that can follow an ibex. And that is the whole point.

Every morning, at first light, Dawa leads her daughter down.

Not all the way. Just to the bottom of the cliffs, where the rock gives way to a slope of grass and herbs, still wet with frost.

And here, they do something strange. They eat as fast as they can. Barely chewing. Tearing up mouthfuls of grass and swallowing them almost whole.

Because the ground is where the hunters are, and they do not stay one second longer than they must.

An hour later, they are back on the cliff. And only now, on a ledge where nothing can reach them, do they finally start to chew.

An ibex is a ruminant. Like a cow, it has a stomach with four chambers. It can swallow its food quickly, and later bring it back up to chew it again in safety. It is how the ibex survives. Eat on the ground. Digest on the cliff.

Because there is one more thing the ibex needs, and it cannot get it on the cliffs.

Salt.

Grass in these mountains has almost no minerals in it. So every few days, the ibex have to visit one of the few places where salt seeps out of the ground. A patch of pale, crusted earth in a gully at the bottom of the valley.

And the hunters know exactly where it is.

Dawa reaches the salt lick in the late afternoon. She stops at the top of the gully for a long time, ears turning, nostrils wide. Then she goes down, her daughter close behind.

They lick at the white crust on the rocks. Heads down. For a few seconds, not watching.

Above them, on a boulder, something grey shifts against the grey stone.

A snow leopard.

She has been lying on that boulder since noon, her smoky coat and black rosettes blending perfectly with the lichen on the rock.

Dawa's head comes up. She has seen nothing. But something is wrong. And an ibex never ignores something wrong.

She gives a sharp whistle, an ibex alarm call that cuts through the thin air like a blade. Her daughter bolts.

The leopard launches off the boulder.

But the ibex are not running away. They are running up. Straight toward the cliff at the head of the gully. Leaping from rock to rock, each jump higher than the last.

The leopard is fast. Faster than the ibex on open ground. But the ground runs out. And the cliff begins.

Dawa hits the rock wall and does not slow down. Up a crack. Onto a ledge. Up again. Ten meters. Twenty. Her daughter right beside her, hooves finding holds on stone that looks perfectly smooth.

The leopard reaches the foot of the cliff and stops.

She looks up. Two ibex are standing on the wall above her, as calm as if they were standing in a field.

She turns, and walks away.

This is why the ibex lives the way it does. A snow leopard is one of the greatest climbers among the big cats. But it hunts by ambush, from above, and on flat or broken ground. If it does not catch an ibex in the first few seconds, before it reaches the cliffs, it almost never catches it at all.

So the snow leopard waits where the ibex must go. The salt licks. The grassy slopes. The places where the cliffs end.

The ground.

As summer comes, the family climbs.

The snow melts upward, and the ibex follow the green line as it rises up the mountain. Higher and higher, until by midsummer they are grazing on meadows more than five thousand meters above the sea, where the air holds half the oxygen it does at the coast.

And for most of the year, the family lives apart.

The males gather in their own herds, on gentler slopes with richer grass, where they can eat as much as possible and grow as big as possible. The females and young stay on the steepest, most broken ground on the mountain, closer to the cliffs, where the grass is poorer, but the kids are safer.

For a mother, it is a simple trade. Less food. More life.

Look closely at his horns. Each year, an ibex's horns grow, and each winter, the growth slows and leaves a ring. Count the rings, and you can read the age of an ibex the way you read a tree. Kesang has eleven.

On the front of each horn are thick ridges, like knuckles. And soon, he will be using them.

At the start of winter, as the first heavy snow falls, the males come down off their slopes and go looking for the females. The rut has begun.

It is the only time of the year the family is together. And for the males, it is a battle.

Two males face each other on a slope above the herd. They walk side by side, slowly, chins raised, showing off the length of their horns. Most of the time, this is enough. The smaller male backs down.

But not always.

The two males rear up on their hind legs, standing taller than a man. Hang there, perfectly balanced. And drop, slamming their horns together with the whole weight of their bodies.

The crack echoes off the cliffs. Again. And again.

An ibex can take blows like this because its skull is built like a crash helmet, with a double layer of bone over the brain. And the ridges on the horns lock together, so the force goes down into the thick muscles of the neck.

Kesang wins. And now comes the strangest part of the rut.

He does not charge at Dawa. He creeps toward her, low to the ground, his neck stretched out, flicking his tongue in and out. Then he stops, lifts his head, and curls back his upper lip in a strange, frozen grimace.

He is not snarling. He is tasting the air.

That grimace pulls scent into a special organ in the roof of his mouth. It tells him whether Dawa is ready to breed. And she will only be ready for a day or two in the entire year. Miss it, and he waits another twelve months.

For weeks, Kesang barely eats. He spends every hour guarding the females and fighting off rivals. By the end of the rut, he will have burned away much of the fat he spent all summer building.

Winter in the Karakoram is long and brutal. Temperatures fall to thirty below. Snow buries the high meadows, and the ibex are driven lower down the mountain.

But they do not go down into the deep snow of the valleys. In snow up to its chest, an ibex cannot run, and cannot climb. On flat ground in winter, it is helpless.

So instead, the family moves onto the steep slopes that face the sun. Slopes so steep that the snow slides off them, or is blown away by the wind, leaving the grass underneath bare. On these slopes, they can feed, and still be a few leaps from the cliffs.

Their coats have changed too. Under the long outer hair, they now grow a layer of fine, soft wool, one of the warmest natural fibres on Earth. It traps the heat so well that snow can settle on an ibex's back without melting.

But for one old ibex, the winter is too much.

He is older than Kesang, thin and stiff, his horns heavy with fourteen rings. And in the deep cold, he has started feeding far from the cliffs, where the snow is thinner.

And the snow leopard is there.

She has been watching the old male for three days. She has learned his path. Down the gully at dawn, out onto the flat ground, back to the cliffs before noon.

That morning, she does not wait on a boulder. She waits in the snow, flat, behind a ridge, between him and the cliffs.

He walks out onto the flat ground. Lowers his head to dig for grass.

She comes over the ridge at a sprint.

He sees her and runs for the cliffs. But she is already between them. He turns, and runs the only way left. Down the slope, onto the flat.

On the open ground, the snow leopard is faster. Fifty meters. Twenty.

She leaps, and lands on his back.

He staggers, and goes down in the snow.

The old ibex did nothing wrong. He was simply too far from the rock. A few hundred meters from the cliffs, on ground he should never have been on.

And up on the cliffs, the rest of the herd watches from the ledges. Safe.

Through the whole of that winter, Dawa has been carrying a secret.

She is pregnant. Inside her, a kid has been growing since the rut. It will take almost six months, through the coldest, hungriest season of the year.

As the snow begins to melt and the first green returns to the slopes, Dawa does something she never does.

She leaves the herd.

Alone, she climbs. Past the ledges where the herd rests. Past the cliffs where the leopard cannot follow. Up to the steepest, most broken rock on the whole mountain, to a ledge barely wide enough to lie on.

No wolf could reach this ledge. No leopard would try. Only the eagles can see it.

And it is here, in early summer, alone, that she gives birth.

The kid arrives wet, folded and tiny. Barely three kilos. Dawa licks it clean, nudging it with her nose. And within an hour, on a ledge three hundred meters above the valley, the kid is standing.

Its legs are shaking. Its hooves are soft. But they are already ibex hooves, with the same hard rims and rubbery pads as its mother's.

For the first few days, the kid stays tucked among the rocks on the ledge, while Dawa feeds close by. Then it follows her. And within days of being born, the kid is climbing.

Down a crack. Across a ledge. Up a slab of rock that would stop a human climber. Every step a little surer than the last.

This is the most surprising thing of all about an ibex. It does not learn to climb. It is born knowing how. It only learns where.

A week later, Dawa brings the kid back to the herd.

Other mothers have given birth on other ledges, and now the kids gather together in a group. A nursery on the side of a cliff. While some of the mothers feed, one or two stay behind, watching over all the kids at once.

And the kids play.

They chase each other along ledges as wide as a hand. They leap onto boulders and spring straight off again. They rear up on their hind legs and bump heads, exactly as their fathers did in the rut.

It looks like a game. But every leap is practice. Every chase is training for the day a leopard comes.

And up here, the danger comes from the sky. A golden eagle circles over the nursery, looking for a kid that has strayed too far from the rock.

The moment its shadow crosses the cliff, the mothers whistle, and every kid presses itself against the stone. The eagle passes over. And the game begins again.

At the end of the summer, the kid takes its first trip down to the ground.

At dawn, it follows Dawa off the cliff and onto the slope of grass below, where it has never been before. Flat, open ground, stretching away in every direction.

It eats beside her. Fast. Heads down. Then heads up, every few seconds, watching the boulders.

And when Dawa turns back toward the cliff, the kid does not need to be told. It follows her up the rock, leaping from ledge to ledge, until the two of them are standing high above the valley again, where nothing can reach them.

This is the life of the ibex. Every day, down to the ground for the things it needs. And every day, back up to the cliffs to live.

The ground feeds them. The cliffs keep them alive.

Today, the Himalayan ibex still survives across the great mountain ranges of Central and South Asia. In parts of the Karakoram, local communities now protect the ibex, and the herds have been growing again.

But the mountains are changing. The winters are warming, the snow line is climbing, and the green meadows are shifting higher every year. The ibex, and the snow leopard that hunts it, are being squeezed into less and less of the mountain.

Perhaps the creator gave the ibex the cliffs, so that even the gentlest animal on the mountain would always have one place where nothing could follow.

Tonight, somewhere in the Karakoram, a mother ibex lies on a ledge above the world, her kid asleep against her side. Below them, in the dark, the snow leopard is walking the ground.

And up here, they are exactly where they belong.
